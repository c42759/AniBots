## 1. What the Numbers Couldn’t Tell Them

Kura had qualified for the World Cup, but the result did not make him feel ready. The final match against Soren replayed in his mind whenever he looked at the recording: Tigris-Fury’s clean footwork, its sudden changes of direction, the force behind each strike. Zerdata V2 had won its last-chance bracket, but there were still gaps in the new chassis, and Kura knew there would be stronger AniBots at the Cup.

The first weeks after qualification had taken Kura and Zerdata back through the recording of their defeat and into practice at the workshop and the District 5 hall. Reviewing the match with Luk and Anda had exposed mistakes that a stronger chassis could not correct. Isabell’s positioning exercises had made Kura think more carefully about what he needed to protect. Yet in sparring, he still called Zerdata away from close exchanges before it could finish a counter.

They continued with movement checks, short bouts against Luk and Kabuto-Horn, and timing drills with Anda. Kura watched every diagnostic reading after each test.

On paper, Zerdata looked competitive. Its response time, power delivery, and structural integrity were now within the range of a production AniBot. The acoustic synchronization system gave it a kind of awareness most modern frames did not have.

But paper did not show the whole match.

Kura ran a combat simulation using the data from Zerdata’s first body and Tigris-Fury’s known profile. The result appeared on the screen: Zerdata, 37 percent. Tigris-Fury, 48 percent. The estimate did not include either Handler’s battle experience.

Kura leaned back from the monitor. “Even before we account for Soren, we’re behind.”

“The Primal Series is built for close combat,” Anda said. He had been studying the projected impact curves. “Tigris-Fury’s actuators and armor are tuned for acceleration and striking force. Production-level readings don’t automatically make the machines equivalent.”

Luk leaned over the opposite side of the table. “A simulation can’t measure the person giving the commands.”

“It can measure how quickly the AniBot responds to them,” Anda replied.

“And it can’t measure what happens when we change the plan halfway through,” Luk said. “Kura got past Tigris’s guard by making it chase. He can find an opening again.”

Kura looked at the model. “I don’t want to count on Soren making the same mistake twice.”

Isabell stood beside Swan-Grace near the practice mat, listening. “Then don’t build the plan around one mistake. Build it around what Zerdata can do that Tigris can’t predict.”

Kura turned toward Zerdata. Its new frame was sleek but still unmistakably its own: sturdy limbs, a carefully balanced torso, acoustic sensor fins that shifted with every sound in the room.

“We’ve been treating the harmonic pulse like a warning system,” Kura said. “It hears the movement, predicts the strike, and gets you clear.”

“That is a useful function,” Zerdata said.

“But it can do more,” Kura continued. “A short pulse could interrupt an opponent’s balance or throw off their timing.”

“The pulse is more reliable at close range,” Zerdata said. “If I wait for the right moment to use it, I can create the opening myself.”

“That means going closer. Tigris wins at close range.”

“If I only evade, I let Tigris control the pace.”

The disagreement was not new. Kura wanted Zerdata to stay mobile and avoid taking a heavy hit. Zerdata believed its harmonic ability could disrupt an attacker directly, allowing it to fight back rather than spend the match retreating. They had trained the same ability in two different directions and were beginning to frustrate one another over it.

Luk set Kabuto-Horn’s practice guard on the mat. “When I use Kabuto-Horn’s weight, I don’t have to choose between blocking and moving forward. Sometimes it catches the hit and uses the push to turn the other bot.”

Anda nodded. “The same capability can serve two purposes if the timing is precise. Your problem is treating defense and attack as separate sequences.”

Isabell lifted Swan-Grace’s small sensor wand. “We can test the pulse against different sounds. If Zerdata can tell a real attack from the echo in a crowded arena, it won’t have to use the pulse too early.”

“And I can give it a moving target,” Luk said. “Kabuto-Horn can keep it under pressure while you test.”

Kura looked at the three of them. “All right. Let’s find out what the ability can actually do.”

For the next several days, they trained in short, controlled rounds. Luk sent Kabuto-Horn forward with shield-first charges. Zerdata used its acoustic sensors to read the shifts in Kabuto-Horn’s joints, but Kura called for it to dodge before the opening was clear. Zerdata obeyed, then complained that it had lost a chance to counter.

“You were too close,” Kura said after one round.

“I was close enough to interrupt the charge.”

“And if it had connected?”

“Then the pulse would have redirected the impact.”

“You don’t know that.”

“Neither do you.”

Kura went quiet. Zerdata’s optics dimmed a fraction.

Isabell walked over, lowering the scanner. “You’re both trying to keep the other safe.”

“That isn’t the same as being right,” Anda said.

“No,” she agreed. “But you can test both ideas without making either one the whole plan.”

The next round, Kura waited. Kabuto-Horn charged. Zerdata shifted aside at the last moment, let the heavier AniBot’s shoulder pass, then released a brief harmonic burst against the armored side plate. The pulse did not knock Kabuto-Horn down, but it made the AniBot’s next step land half a beat late. Zerdata used that pause to tap its back and retreat safely.

Luk whistled. “That was it.”

“The pulse disrupted its balance,” Anda said. “And you didn’t waste it.”

Kura faced Zerdata. “You were right about using it offensively.”

“You were right that I needed an opening.”

“Maybe the ability doesn’t have to be one thing,” Isabell said. “It can help you hear an attack coming, then help you change what happens next.”

Zerdata turned its head toward her. “That is an effective description.”

They recalibrated the harmonic array and adjusted two small components in the new chassis: a sensor baffle to reduce interference from surrounding noise, and a flexible support around the pulse emitter to keep the resonance stable during a sudden turn. The changes were modest. They did not make Zerdata stronger than Tigris-Fury. They made Zerdata’s choices clearer.

The updated simulation gave Zerdata a 44 percent chance against Tigris-Fury. Tigris remained at 48, still without either Handler’s experience factored in.

“Seven points,” Luk said. “That’s something.”

“It’s a model,” Anda reminded him.

“And now it’s a model that’s less wrong.”

Kura saved the data. He was still uneasy about Soren’s words at the qualifiers. The mention of Magnus had not sounded like a random taunt. If Soren knew about Ty’s work, then the connection reached farther than the tournament.

That night, Kura brought the subject up with Raya while they locked the workshop.

“I think Soren knows more about Zerdata than he should.”

Raya’s expression became serious. “Did he say something?”

“He mentioned Dad. Then Magnus. Like he wanted to see how I’d react.”

“That sounds deliberate.”

“I want to know how they’re connected.”

Raya glanced toward the office, where Ty’s notebooks were stored. “Then look into it carefully. You don’t have to rush into the first answer you find.”

Kura knew she was remembering the sensor unit he had recovered with Isabell in the wasteland—the old research mark and his father’s initials. He had not yet found a way to place the location code. Now it seemed to point in the same direction as Soren’s provocation.

## 2. The Trail Behind Soren

Kura began with what he could verify. The qualifiers had left a public record of Soren’s Aegis sponsorship, the special equipment assigned to his team, and the private maintenance crews that serviced Tigris-Fury. The chassis was a Primal Series machine, manufactured by Aegis. Its advanced components were not suspicious on their own.

The supply trail was.

Several replacement parts listed in Aegis’s public inventory had no matching delivery records. A contractor named in a shipping manifest had been investigated for moving unlicensed AniChip modifications through District 6. Another name appeared in a ledger Kura found among Ty’s archived research notes: a broker whose company no longer existed but whose shell firms still handled freight near Rust-Ridge.

Anda checked the corporate filings. Luk searched public tournament and freight records. Isabell compared the route data to the signal map recovered from the old sensor unit. Kura traced the hardware serials and found the same strange gap repeated across multiple shipments.

“These parts enter the Aegis network through certified suppliers,” Anda said, displaying the route. “Then a portion of each shipment disappears before inspection.”

“And reappears here,” Kura said, pointing to a string of unregistered depots on the edge of the Badlands.

Luk leaned in. “So Soren’s machine uses illegal parts?”

“We don’t know that,” Kura said. “His team’s maintenance records could be legitimate.”

Isabell studied the route on the screen. “When I started following the supplier names, I searched the ones that didn’t match Aegis’s public records. That’s where I first found the name Anode—on the open networks and in old news reports. They’re a criminal syndicate based in the Rust-Ridge Badlands. They control scrap depots, move illegal hardware, and run unlicensed AniBot combat rings called the Pit.”

Luk glanced from her to the route. “So the missing shipments could be going straight into their territory.”

“But someone around Aegis is moving restricted hardware to Anode.” Isabell studied the route. “That’s what we can prove.”

It was enough to explain why Kura had begun receiving odd messages after the tournament. One was a blank call from an unregistered number. Another was a short warning sent to the workshop’s public repair account: *Stop asking about Rust-Ridge.*

Raya wanted him to report it. Kura agreed to share the evidence with a district investigator, but he also knew official channels could take weeks to reach the Badlands. If Anode was using Aegis contacts to track old components, the recovered records might disappear before anyone looked.

He did not go alone. Luk insisted on coming. Anda said he would join because the evidence was incomplete. Isabell chose to accompany them after confirming the trip with Luk and Raya.

“I can read the sensor traces and keep our communications clear,” she said. “And Swan-Grace can help if we run into trouble.”

Kura felt the familiar urge to tell her it could be dangerous, then stopped himself. Isabell had shown she could handle herself. “All right. We’ll go together, and we’ll stay together.”

They traveled west beyond the eco-city boundary. The green belts thinned into dry plains. Old rail lines ran beside the road, and the horizon was broken by dismantled towers, black rock ridges, and the distant smoke of scrap furnaces. Rust-Ridge began where the city’s clean power grid ended.

The first Anode patrol found them near an abandoned checkpoint. Their AniBots were rugged, heavily plated, and fitted with mismatched parts. One lifted a rifle-like launcher toward the transport.

Luk stepped out first, Kabuto-Horn landing beside him with a heavy clang. “We’re passing through.”

“Nobody passes through without paying,” the operator called.

Kura kept his hands visible. “We’re looking for work at the Pit.”

The operator laughed. “Then you’ll pay with a match.”

The patrol sent two AniBots forward. Kabuto-Horn braced against the first rush, caught the attacker around its shoulders, and threw it sideways into a bank of soft sand. Zerdata moved low beneath the second bot’s swing and struck the inside of its knee. Swan-Grace leapt up, drew the machine’s attention with a quick aerial feint, and landed behind it. The three AniBots forced the patrol back without seriously damaging their machines.

When the operators saw they were outmatched, they lowered their weapons and let the transport continue.

“That was our welcome,” Isabell said, checking Swan-Grace’s wing joints.

“The city rules don’t carry this far,” Anda said. “We’ll need to be careful about what we say and who hears it.”

Kura looked toward the rust-colored ridges. “Then we keep our story simple.”

## 3. The Pit

The Pit was built beneath a disused freight exchange, a maze of concrete tunnels converted into an illegal combat circuit. Music and crowd noise rolled through the entrance. Handlers from the Badlands and beyond gathered around the central ring, trading bets and repair parts beneath flickering red lights.

Vera “Viper” Lin watched from a high balcony. Her arms were bare, a viper tattoo curling down her left one. She had the stillness of someone who never needed to raise her voice to be heard.

“A city kid with an old-looking AniBot,” she said when Kura registered. “The Pit has had stranger entertainment.”

Kura gave the name and match record he had prepared. He did not mention the World Cup. In the Badlands, that would attract the wrong kind of attention.

“You want to fight against stronger AniBots?” Vera asked.

“Yes. I need opponents I haven’t already practised against in my district,” Kura said, keeping the investigation to himself.

She smiled without warmth. “Win your way through the cards, and you might interest me enough to fight you myself.”

Kura faced three opponents over the next two nights. The first used a pair of reinforced claws to overwhelm Zerdata at close range. Kura kept his partner moving, using the acoustic sensors to detect the machine’s feints and choosing moments to counter. Zerdata landed one clean strike to the opponent’s shoulder, followed by a sweep that put it on the mat.

The second match was against a ranged unit that fired bursts of heated pellets. Zerdata could hear the launch mechanism wind up, but the crowded arena distorted its read. Isabell’s earlier calibration helped: Zerdata filtered the crowd echo and tracked the repeated pitch of the weapon’s feeder. It dodged the bursts, closed the gap, and disarmed the opponent with a sharp hit to its launcher arm.

The third fight was harder. A broad, shielded AniBot tried to pin Zerdata against the ring wall. Kura called for a harmonic pulse too early. The opponent’s armor absorbed most of it, and Zerdata took a heavy shoulder hit.

“Don’t force it!” Luk shouted from their corner.

Kura changed the plan. “Let it push. Wait for the second step.”

Zerdata gave ground, listening. The opponent slammed into its guard once, twice. On the second shove, Zerdata pivoted, sent the harmonic pulse into the exposed hinge beneath the shield, and stepped aside. The enemy’s arm locked for a fraction of a second. That was enough for Zerdata to hook the shield edge and wrench the machine off balance.

It landed on its back and tried to rise. Its arms twitched once, then its optics went dark. The machine had been knocked out. Its Handler stepped into the ring and called the fight, and the crowd moved on to the next bout.

Kura’s success brought him back to Vera’s attention. She sent a message through the Pit’s match system: *Final bout. You and me. Let’s see what your machine can really do.*

“She’s the ringmaster,” Luk said. “She’s not offering this because she likes you.”

“I know.”

“Could be a trap,” Anda said.

“It is a trap,” Kura replied. “We just need to know what kind.”

Isabell looked unsettled. “You don’t have to accept.”

Kura met her eyes. “We need the records. And if we don’t accept, Vera still controls what we see.”

Isabell nodded, trusting his judgment even though she disliked the risk. “Then we’ll be ready.”

## 4. Vera’s Challenge

The final bout drew the largest crowd yet. The Pit’s central ring had been cleared of debris, leaving the metal floor scarred and stained. Vera stood opposite Kura with Serpent-Coil coiled beside her. The AniBot’s green armor and red accents caught the harsh overhead lights. Its long tail rested in a loose circle, ready to spring.

“This place doesn’t care about your feelings, boy!” Vera said. “Out here, your shiny rules don’t matter.”

“I’m not here to prove anything to the crowd,” Kura replied. “I only want to get stronger.”

“Very well.”

Vera stepped toward the ring. The surrounding crowd pressed closer, hungry for the fight. On the far side, Anda caught Isabell’s eye and gave the smallest nod. They had spotted a service entrance on the archive map. As soon as the crowd’s attention fixed on the ring, they would slip away and look for the logs. Luk noticed their signal and shifted toward the team rail, ready to cover their absence.

A harsh buzzer sounded from the wall.

Serpent-Coil surged forward, its tail driving it across the floor with a low, fast glide. Zerdata stepped out of the first constriction, but the tail curled around its ankle and yanked. Zerdata hit the floor and rolled before the bot could wrap it completely.

Kura called for distance. Before Zerdata could get clear, Serpent-Coil slid through a gap in the floor barriers, coming at it from the side. A thin mist sprayed from its forearm. It struck the metal where Zerdata had been standing and ate into the surface with a sharp hiss.

“Corrosive compound,” Luk called from the team rail. “Don’t let it coat the joints!”

Kura pulled Zerdata back. “Keep to the dry section!”

Vera used the ring itself against them. Serpent-Coil’s tail swept debris across the floor, blocking the open path. Then it slithered beneath a raised panel, appearing behind Zerdata and wrapping around its waist.

The coils tightened. Zerdata’s torso armor creaked.

The fight stretched on. Zerdata escaped one coil only to have Serpent-Coil catch its leg again. Kura kept calling for it to break away, but each retreat gave Vera room to reshape the ring with the tail—dragging loose barriers into their path, spraying corrosive mist across the floor, and forcing Zerdata toward the outer wall. Its armor picked up several shallow scars. Serpent-Coil had taken a hard strike to its side, but remained quick, coiling and uncoiling in sharp bursts.

While all eyes followed the fight, Anda and Isabell slipped through the service door. Swan-Grace followed close, silent on the concrete. They moved down a narrow passage to the archive terminal and began searching the shipment logs, careful not to trigger the security alerts. Luk stayed at the rail, drawing the attention of anyone who glanced toward their empty places by shouting advice at the battle.

“Use the sound to create an opening,” Luk’s voice came through Kura’s comm, low beneath the crowd’s roar.

Kura felt panic rising. He had asked Zerdata to use the pulse exactly as he once had in training: a desperate way to escape. It had bought them time, but Vera kept changing tactics. “You’re waiting for a clean opening,” she taunted. “There aren’t any here.”

Kura heard Luk’s words again and understood. Zerdata did not need to wait for a perfect opening. It could make one—but it needed to draw Serpent-Coil into the right position first.

“Zerdata, let it catch you!”

“Confirming?”

“I trust you. Let it catch you, then fire the cannon.”

Serpent-Coil shot forward. Zerdata raised its left arm as if to block, then let the elbow go loose. The tail wrapped around it and tightened, dragging Zerdata across the floor toward the wall. Vera gave a sharp pull, confident she had trapped it.

“Now!”

Zerdata braced its feet against the wall and turned its left forearm, bringing the Sonic Pulse Cannon against the coil wrapped around it. “Sonic Pulse Cannon!” A concentrated wave burst from the arm-mounted emitter at point-blank range and traveled through Serpent-Coil’s tail and torso. The metal floor shivered beneath them. Serpent-Coil’s coils spasmed and released. Its body went rigid, its optics blinking without focus.

For several seconds, Vera’s AniBot did not respond.

Zerdata used that moment. It pushed off the wall, twisted free of the slack tail, and lunged before Serpent-Coil’s systems could recover. Its right hand unfolded into the Resonant Strike Claw. The claw swept across Serpent-Coil’s visor with a sharp, ringing impact. The visor shattered. Serpent-Coil’s head snapped back, its limbs jerked, and its optics went dark. It collapsed to the floor, unresponsive.

The crowd fell silent. Vera stared at Serpent-Coil, then keyed its emergency shutdown from her wrist unit. When the AniBot remained motionless, she stepped forward and raised a hand. “The fight is over.”

Kura let out the breath he had been holding. Zerdata lowered its arm, the Resonant Strike Claw retracting with a soft mechanical click.

Vera stared at Zerdata, then at Kura. “Interesting strategy, boy!”

“Thanks.”

She held his gaze, then gave a small nod. “You won.”

At that moment, Anda and Isabell slipped back into view with Swan-Grace. Isabell held a data wafer in one hand. Luk saw them and gave Kura a quick nod from the rail.

“We found the shipment manifests and the restricted archive,” Anda said quietly. “No one noticed us.”

The data wafer contained maintenance manifests, smuggling records, and a list of old components purchased through shell suppliers. The same Aegis-linked contractor appeared beside Anode’s transport schedules. Anda had found a second file hidden in the archive’s search index, with requests tied to rare AniChips.

“These aren’t just parts,” he said, reading the file. “They’re looking for Generation 0 cores. Worldwide.”

The group went quiet.

Kura stared at the words on the screen. He thought of Soren saying Ty’s name. He thought of the damaged sensor, his father’s initials, and Zerdata’s core.

“Magnus knows about Zerdata,” Isabell said.

“Or suspects what it is,” Anda replied. “Either way, this makes the qualifier match more than a rivalry.”

They had copied the manifests and the search requests, but the archive held more records than they could carry. As the group reviewed the files beside the service passage, an alarm began to pulse behind them. The terminal had finally flagged the access. A guard noticed the opened console, and Vera appeared at the corridor entrance with two enforcers.

“Funny, boy. So you weren’t only here to fight stronger AniBots,” she said. “Now leave before Magnus hears you were here.”

“You’re letting us go?” Luk asked.

“I’m letting you leave while I still can.” Her expression was unreadable. “Don’t make me regret it.”

Kura copied the files he could and followed his friends out through a maintenance passage. They had the evidence, but it only confirmed what they already feared: the operation led back to Magnus’s Citadel.

## 5. The Citadel Engine

The Citadel stood at the center of Anode territory, a fortress of repurposed industrial towers and armored platforms. Power lines ran from it toward the surrounding settlements. Its core engine supplied much of the syndicate’s communications, weapons, and machinery.

Kura and his friends could not simply walk through the front gate. They waited for nightfall and followed a service route Isabell had found in the stolen maintenance files. Swan-Grace scouted from above. Ancient-Wyrm watched the ridge. Kabuto-Horn and Zerdata moved with the others through a drainage conduit leading to the outer service yard.

They encountered armed salvage units near the first power relay. Kabuto-Horn took the lead, catching a heavy strike on its guard and shoving the attacker into a stack of empty drums. Zerdata ducked beneath a second machine’s swing and struck its knee joint. Isabell and Swan-Grace kept watch over the passage while Anda disabled the relay’s alarm sensor.

“That only buys us a few minutes,” Anda said.

“Then we’ll use them,” Kura answered.

They reached the engine chamber. The machine filled the lower level, a massive turbine and power regulator assembly surrounded by heat exchangers and cable trunks. It fed the Citadel’s systems and kept the outer yards lit. Control stations ringed the chamber, with security guards moving between them.

The team split. Luk and Kabuto-Horn drew the guards away from the lower walkway. Anda and Ancient-Wyrm used the distraction to reach the archive terminal. Isabell and Swan-Grace guarded the maintenance door while Kura and Zerdata approached the engine controls.

“We can’t blow this whole place apart,” Kura said. “There are settlements connected to the grid.”

“We don’t need to,” Anda replied over comms. “The regulator has a manual overload path. If you interrupt the synchronization and isolate the output, the engine will shut down safely.”

“How long?”

“If I read the diagram correctly, two minutes.”

“And if you’re wrong?”

“Then it will shut down less safely.”

Kura and Zerdata moved toward the regulator housing. A security AniBot dropped from the upper platform, landing between them and the control panel. It was a brute of mismatched armor and heavy limbs, built for blocking corridors.

Zerdata angled to the left. The security unit swung a thick arm. Zerdata slipped outside the strike and hit its elbow, but the opponent turned faster than expected and clipped Zerdata’s side.

“The joint is reinforced,” Kura said. “Don’t hit it again.”

Zerdata listened to the chamber. The engine’s vibration filled the space, masking the security unit’s servos. It had to distinguish the AniBot’s movement from the roar of the machinery.

Isabell’s voice came through the comm. “Use the regulator rhythm. Its steps are off-beat from the engine.”

Zerdata tilted its head. The engine throbbed in a steady sequence. Beneath it, the security bot’s heavy footfalls landed just after each pulse.

“I hear it,” Zerdata said.

The bot charged. Zerdata waited for the next engine pulse, moved on the beat, and let the attack pass by. It turned and struck the bot’s lower back with a short harmonic burst. The security unit stumbled, its movement rhythm disrupted. Kura ran past to the regulator controls.

“Isolated output,” Anda said. “Now.”

Kura pulled the manual lever and cut the synchronization link. The engine’s roar dropped in pitch. Lights across the chamber blinked out in sequence. The enormous turbine slowed, then stopped.

The shutdown was controlled, but not silent. The Citadel’s warning sirens began to howl.

Luk rejoined them, Kabuto-Horn’s shoulder armor dented. “We have to go. The whole place knows we’re here.”

Anda had copied the files. Isabell and Swan-Grace fell back from the door, keeping the guards from reaching the group. They ran for the service conduit as the Citadel’s lights failed one bank at a time.

Kura looked back once. The engine chamber had gone dark, and the security units were powering down as their command network faltered. Across the surrounding yards, the lights went out in a widening arc. Anode’s grip over the territory had taken a serious blow, but the people living in the nearby settlements were not left without power; the team had isolated the Citadel’s systems from the public grid before stopping the engine.

They reached the outer ridge before the first Anode vehicles arrived.

Magnus was nowhere in sight.

## 6. What the Records Pointed To

The Citadel’s shutdown disrupted Anode’s operations across Rust-Ridge. Several illegal depots lost communications, and the Pit’s ledger network went offline. The syndicate was hurt, but not broken. By the time Kura and his friends reached a safe settlement, Magnus had disappeared into the Badlands with Silverback-Smash and a portion of his security force.

The recovered files survived. Anda had copied the full search records before the archive terminal went dark. They showed that Anode had spent years tracking Generation 0 AniChips and purchasing old research components wherever they surfaced. One list included fragments from the same research program as the sensor Kura had found in the wasteland.

Another file contained a reference to Zerdata’s core family: Vulpes Zerda.

Kura stared at the line, feeling the pieces shift into a shape he could not yet understand.

“Soren wasn’t just trying to beat me,” he said. “He wanted to know what Zerdata was. Maybe he wanted the core.”

“The files don’t prove he knew the whole story,” Anda said. “But he was connected to people searching for it.”

“Then we find out who told him,” Luk said.

Kura thought of Dr. Thorne at the qualifiers, looking at Zerdata as if the old machine had answered a question he had carried for years. He thought of Ty’s research marks and the unknown location code.

“This leads to the old research records,” Kura said. “Maybe to Research Center Zero.”

Isabell stepped beside him. “We’ll go with you.”

Kura looked at her. This time he did not pretend he had not been hoping she would say it.

“I’d like that.”

The journey home took them through the outer settlements and back toward Neo Metropolis. Zerdata’s V2 chassis had held up through the Pit and the Citadel, though several armor plates needed replacement. Kura added the new readings to its maintenance log and saved a separate copy of the Anode files.

When they returned to District 4, Raya was waiting at the workshop. She hugged Kura tightly, then inspected the scuffs on Zerdata’s frame as if deciding which repair to start with.

“You found something?” she asked.

“A lot,” Kura said. “And none of it makes sense yet.”

He laid out the copied records on the table. The reference to Vulpes Zerda glowed beside the old sensor’s matching research stamp. A second file pointed toward a facility that had been absent from current maps for centuries.

Research Center Zero.

The Citadel had gone dark, but the questions it left behind had only grown brighter. Somewhere in the oldest records was the truth about the Ancient cores, about Ty’s work, and about why Anode had searched so long for Zerdata.

Kura looked at his friends. Luk was already studying the location data. Anda had opened a second screen and was checking old maps. Isabell stood close at Kura’s side, Swan-Grace’s wing resting lightly against Zerdata’s shoulder.

“Let’s find the entrance,” Kura said.

Together, they turned toward the next trail.
