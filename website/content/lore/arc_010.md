## 1. The Window Sill

The first thing Kura Ato heard that morning was a gear giving up.

It clicked once beneath his wrench, clicked again, then spun free with a little metallic chirp. Kura caught it before it vanished under the workbench. The customer’s courier drone lay open in front of him, its casing propped like a shell and its right stabilizer hanging by two wires.

“Not dead,” Kura told it. “Just dramatic.”

Across the workshop, his mother looked up from the ledger. “If you can persuade it to fly, I’ll add dramatic to the invoice.”

Kura grinned and set the gear beside the others. The Ato Family Workshop occupied the ground floor of a narrow building in District 4, where green canals ran between salvage yards and the clean transit loop hummed overhead. From the open doors came the smell of warm rain, oil, and the market stalls setting up along the service road. A civic tram glided past beyond the trees, silent as a shadow.

Inside, the workshop was less graceful. A shelf sagged under mismatched motors. Three customers’ machines waited for parts. A leaking roof had been patched with a panel from an old cargo carrier. The ledger in Raya’s hands was neat; the numbers were not.

Kura knew the pattern. He could replace the drone’s stabilizer with a restored unit from the salvage bin, charge fairly, and still have enough left to pay for the workshop’s power allotment. If he could make the part last another season.

“Try this one,” Raya said, sliding a paper-thin slate across the counter. “The courier wants it before the afternoon route.”

“I’ll have it ready.”

“You said that about the irrigation controller.”

“That was a different afternoon.”

Raya gave him a look over the top of the ledger. It was stern for half a second, then softened. “Your father used to say the same thing.”

The small pause that followed was familiar. Four years had passed since Ty Ato vanished. The authorities had searched the rail lines and the outer roads, questioned the people who knew him, and eventually closed their inquiry without finding a body, a vehicle, or an answer. The file said presumed lost. Raya had never called him dead. Kura had learned that grief could live quite comfortably beside hope, neither one willing to leave.

He turned back to the drone. “Then I’ll make sure the part lasts longer than his promises.”

Raya’s smile reached her eyes. “That’s my boy.”

Customers had been trusting him with small repairs for years, first under Ty’s supervision and later with Raya checking the jobs and accounts. He knew what a worn bearing sounded like before a scanner flagged it. On a crowded workbench, he could usually work out which component had failed and why.

He had never had an AniBot partner of his own. Sometimes Luk brought match recordings to the shop, and Kura watched while his hands were busy with a repair. He could point out a dragging joint or a weapon taking too long to reset. When Luk asked what the Handler should do next, Kura was much less certain.

That evening, after the last customer had collected a repaired loader, Kura carried his tools upstairs. The family’s rooms were small, with the workshop’s vibration running through the floorboards. He washed the grease from his hands, set his slate to charge, and opened the window to let in the cool air.

At the end of the short hall stood the door to his father’s home office.

Kura had not opened it in four years. He had passed it every day, sometimes slowing as if he expected to hear Ty moving papers on the other side. After the search ended, Raya had closed the room and left it untouched. The door had become part of the house: familiar, silent, and easier to walk past than to face.

Tonight, something held him there.

He stood with one hand on the knob. He could still go back to his room. Instead, he fetched the small key from the kitchen drawer and returned. The lock turned with a dry click.

Dust and cobwebs filled the office. They veiled the shelves, bridged the old lamp to a stack of manuals, and softened the outlines of tools left exactly where Ty had set them down. The room smelled of paper, metal, and years of closed air.

On top of the wooden desk lay a notebook.

Kura brushed away the dust with his sleeve. The worn cover read **T.S. ATO — RESEARCH LOG — PROTOTYPES / NOTES**. His thumb traced his father’s initials. He had seen Ty’s handwriting in workshop labels and old holiday cards, but never on a book that had been waiting for him.

![T.S. Ato research log](/images/lore/arc_010/kura_lore_1.jpg)

He opened it.

The first pages were filled with diagrams: a fox-shaped AniBot frame, acoustic arrays, spring-loaded legs, and a core slot set between the shoulder blades. A folded note had been tucked into the notebook. Kura recognized the handwriting before he had unfolded it.

> Son,  
> I’m sorry I can’t give him to you myself. I believe he will protect you.  
> His name is Zerdata.
> —Your father,
> Ty Ato

![Father's message](/images/lore/arc_010/kura_lore_2.jpg)

Kura read the words once, then again. The room seemed to draw in around him. His father had written to him. Not in a log or a technical warning, but in a note meant to be found.

Something small rested against the bottom edge of the page. A cartridge with a cloudy transparent shell and gold contact pins. Beneath the surface, an orange light pulsed like a tiny, steady heartbeat. Its label read **VULPES ZERDA — G0-004**.

![Vulpes Zerda Chip](/images/lore/arc_010/kura_lore_3.jpg)

Kura lifted it carefully. The warmth of it surprised him.

The notebook diagrams pointed toward a shape in the far corner. A sheet covered a completed AniBot chassis, and dust and webs clung to its shoulders and broad, fox-like ears. Kura drew the cover away. Orange and white armor emerged beneath the grime, battered-looking beside the polished frames in the shop catalogues but built with a solidity that made him think of the old machines Ty had repaired by hand. Exposed dampeners and heavy alloy plates formed a lean, spring-loaded body. The dish-shaped ears were large enough to catch the smallest vibration.

The machine had been waiting in the dark for four years.

Kura found the recessed core slot between its shoulder blades. The note trembled in his hand. He looked at the glowing cartridge, then at the silent frame his father had built for it.

“Zerdata,” he whispered.

![4yo AniBot](/images/lore/arc_010/kura_lore_4.jpg)

The name seemed to belong in the room.

He set the notebook open on the desk and eased the chip into the slot. It seated with a firm click. Orange light ran along the frame’s old bus lines, briefly illuminating the cobwebs. One ear twitched.

Kura pulled his hand away. The chassis remained still, its light fading back to a pulse.

![AniBot Start!](/images/lore/arc_010/kura_lore_5.jpg)

He had no diagnostic shield connected, no thermal readings, and no idea what the first surge might do. He took the cartridge out again and held it close, as if the warmth might answer him.

Behind him, Ty’s note lay open beside the blueprints. On the page, his father had already given the machine its name.

## 2. A Voice in the Static

Kura looked at the dark frame, then at the notebook and the line Ty had written beneath its name.

“I can give it a look downstairs,” he muttered, dusting off his hands. “All my diagnostic gear and heavy power leads are in the shop.”

Raya was already waiting below, finishing the workshop accounts at the counter. Kura went back to the office and tested the chassis balance before he tried to move it. It was compact, but built from dense alloy and thick mechanical plates. He bent his knees, hoisted the machine onto his back, and gritted his teeth as its weight settled across his shoulders.

“Careful on the stairs,” Raya called up.

“I’ve got it.”

The narrow wooden steps creaked beneath him. Kura took them one at a time, keeping a hand on the rail and the other braced against the frame. At the bottom, he lowered the chassis flat across his primary metal workbench. The bench groaned but held.

Raya studied the fox-like silhouette beneath its dust. “Ty built this?”

“He finished it. I think.” Kura glanced at the notebook tucked under his arm. “There’s a lot I don’t understand yet.”

He found the maintenance port beneath the neck collar and connected his handheld diagnostic tablet with an optical data cable. The power cell was isolated. He checked the leads once more, set a conservative current limit, and keyed the ignition sequence.

The tablet flickered. Hex dumps and allocation faults flooded the screen, followed by timeout warnings.

```text
DIAGNOSTIC PROBE: INITIALIZED
CORE CONNECTION: UNSTABLE (HIGH IMPEDANCE)
NEURAL SECTOR: 0x00A7F... READ FAILURE
ROM REGION: PARTITION LOCKED
```

“Neural sector is a name for the bot’s internal processing bank,” Kura said, noticing Raya’s worried look. “It’s not connected to a person.”

He spent the next hours trying to coax a clean signal from the old architecture. He adjusted bypass jumpers, cleaned oxidized contacts with solvent, and probed the power distribution bus with his multimeter. Raya brought him food once, then a fresh mug of tea. Kura forgot both beside the tablet.

The afternoon faded into dusk. The primary kernel still refused to load.

Rubbing his exhausted eyes, Kura leaned back from the bench. “Old architecture. The flash blocks are scattered all over the storage sectors.”

“Can you fix it?” Raya asked.

“I can try to rebuild the map. Low voltage, slow cycle. It might take all night.”

“Will it be safe?”

“It’s safer than forcing the locked partition. If anything overheats, the cutoff will shut it down.”

He configured a low-voltage memory defragmentation and rebuild cycle on the tablet, setting it to loop overnight. The display warned that the process might restore only a fallback configuration. Kura checked the thermal cutoff twice before accepting.

“Let’s see if your memory can stitch itself together by morning,” he murmured.

He turned off the shop lights, pulled down the front metal security shutter, and locked up for the night.

At 02:47, the workshop stood in silence, lit only by the green pulse of the tablet monitor. Its progress bar flickered, stalled for a breath, then reached the end.

```text
MEMORY RE-BLOCKING DEFRAGMENTATION: 99.8% ... 100% COMPLETE
INTEGRITY RESTRUCTURED
```

A sharp electric click echoed through the empty shop.

The fennec crest on the chip flared bright gold. Twin cyan optics sparked to life beneath the visor, casting cold light across the tools on the bench. A synthesized metallic voice sounded from the chassis speaker, quiet at first, then clearer as the system came online.

```text
[SYSTEM]: BIOS configuration restored.
[SYSTEM]: Initializing boot sequence...
[SYSTEM]: Booting... 1%... 14%... 48%... 89%... 100% — COMPLETE.
[SYSTEM]: Memory diagnostic complete. No critical hardware faults found.
[SYSTEM]: Voice recognition system: ONLINE
[SYSTEM]: Resonant subsystems: ONLINE
[SYSTEM]: Navigation and gyroscope sensors: ONLINE
[SYSTEM]: User profile loading... ERROR. User configuration corrupted.
[SYSTEM]: Memory integrity check... ERROR.
[SYSTEM]: CRITICAL: ROM segment corrupted. Memory re-synchronization required.
[SYSTEM]: Fallback protocol active. Default settings loaded.
[SYSTEM]: STATUS: READY.
[SYSTEM]: WAITING TO CONNECT WITH USER...
[SYSTEM]: Connection failed. Target not in range. Waiting...
```

The cyan optics dimmed to a steady, rhythmic glow. The AniBot remained still on the workbench, awaiting a handler.

Morning bells were just beginning to ring across District 4 when Kura unlocked the shop. He stepped behind the counter, tossed his jacket onto a stool, and glanced toward the workbench.

He stopped.

The AniBot’s head was upright. Its visor glowed cyan.

Kura hurried to the bench and pulled the diagnostic tablet toward him. The event log showed the overnight reboot:

```text
[02:48:12] BIOS RESTORED.
[02:49:03] USER CONFIGURATION CORRUPTED. DEFAULT MATRIX ACTIVE.
[02:49:40] STATUS: WAITING FOR USER TO CONNECT...
```

“Wait... what?” Kura gasped. “It actually booted?”

The machine’s head made a small adjustment, tracking him with subtle servo clicks. On the slate, a message blinked: **AWAITING HANDLER LINK**.

Kura reached into his utility belt and took out his wireless handler-link earbud. People still called it a neural link out of habit, but it carried only voice and encrypted data; direct brain-to-machine links had been forbidden for generations. He fitted the earbud and pressed its pairing button.

A clean chime sounded in his ear.

```text
“Connecting to user...”
“New user initialization detected. Please state your name.”
```

Kura swallowed. “Kura.”

```text
“User detected. Welcome, Kura.”
“Please state AniBot designation.”
```

He looked down at the open notebook and remembered his father’s cursive note.

“Zerdata,” he said clearly.

```text
“Name accepted. Configuration saved: ZERDATA.”
“User and AniBot pairing profile registered.”
“Starting personality transfer protocol...”
“Transfer complete. Restarting kernel...”
```

The fans spun up with a sharp whine. Zerdata’s head tilted back, and the fennec ear dishes snapped open, twitching toward the smallest sounds in the workshop.

Its voice burst from the speaker, frantic and bewildered.

“What? Where am I? Who are you? Where is Ato?”

The ears turned sharply. “Ato! Where is Ato?!”

Kura stared at the machine. “Wait... Dad?”

Zerdata froze. Its acoustic dishes swiveled toward him and held there.

“Dad? Do you know where Ato is? Where is he?”

Kura felt a pang in his chest. He took a slow breath and lowered his voice. “Ato was my father. His name was Ty. He disappeared four years ago. We never found him.”

Zerdata’s optics flickered. “Disappeared?”

“I don’t know where he is.” Kura’s voice caught. He turned toward the stairs. “Mom! Raya, can you come down?”

Raya appeared at the top of the stairs, then hurried the rest of the way when she saw the glowing eyes on the bench. She stopped beside Kura.

“Is it awake?”

“I think so.” He glanced at the machine. “It remembers Dad. Or something about him.”

Zerdata listened to Raya’s footsteps and the catch in her breath. “You know Ato.”

“I do,” Raya said softly. “I’m Raya. Ty was my husband.”

The optics settled on her. “Where is he?”

Raya’s hand found Kura’s shoulder. For a moment, neither of them answered.

“We don’t know,” Kura said at last. “But we’ll find out.”

The tablet chimed again. A warning filled the screen: **ROM SEGMENT CORRUPTED. MEMORY RE-SYNCHRONIZATION REQUIRED.** Kura opened the diagnostic panel but kept his hands clear of the recovery controls.

“Your memory is damaged,” he told Zerdata. “I can run another scan, but I won’t force open the locked parts.”

“Do not pull at them,” Zerdata said. Its voice had quieted. “It hurts.”

Kura’s fingers left the screen. “I’m sorry.”

A soft orange pulse moved beneath the torso plate. “You stopped.”

“Of course I did.”

Raya looked from the chip to the old notebook lying open on the counter. “Your father left this for you.”

Kura nodded. “He called the machine Zerdata.”

The AniBot turned the name over in its processor. “Zerdata.”

“Do you remember it?”

“No. But it feels familiar.”

“Then we’ll keep it.”

Kura began a careful calibration. Zerdata could stand, take short steps, and track a finger across the shop. Its movements still came in uneven bursts. A shoulder servo overcorrected; one springer fired too quickly, then locked. When Kura tried to pair the tablet again, the connection lagged and dropped. The diagnostic system called the whole assembly incompatible.

“Your frame’s fighting itself,” Kura said.

“Your slate is very loud,” Zerdata answered.

“It’s a diagnostic.”

“It is loud.”

Kura changed the polling rate, softened the connection, and switched the handshake from the modern pulse to the slower carrier signal sketched in Ty’s notebook. Zerdata’s movements settled, a little. It still turned toward sounds before commands, and its responses arrived on a delay that made Kura’s first instructions useless.

“Step left.”

Zerdata stepped right.

“Your other left.”

“I have two.”

“Fair point.”

The calibration lasted until the workshop lights dimmed. They got no further than a slow walk across the shop. The memory locks remained. The chassis warmed beyond the tablet’s safe estimate, and Kura shut down the frame before the thermal cutoff had to do it for him.

At the end of the day, he looked back at the silent machine. “We’ll try again tomorrow.”

From the core slot came a faint orange pulse.

## 3. The Practice Line

Luk arrived the next afternoon with a crate of refurbished motor couplings and no intention of being paid.

“You’re not taking those back,” Kura said.

“I’m not taking money either.” Luccas Tirio set the crate on the workbench. “We’ve had this argument. You lost.”

“I don’t remember losing.”

“You were busy losing to a courier drone.” Luk hooked an arm around his shoulder and grinned. “Where’s the mystery machine?”

Kura glanced at Raya. She answered with a small nod. He led Luk and his younger sister, Isabell, into the study. Isa followed with a rabbit charm clipped to her bag, then stopped when Kura turned around to hold the door.

“Hi,” she said.

“Hey, Isa.”

She looked down at her shoes. “Luk said you had a new AniBot.”

“I did.”

Luk leaned toward Zerdata, now standing under its own power. “That’s the whole introduction? You said it was a mystery machine.”

“It is.”

“Does it have a name?”

“Zerdata.”

The fox-shaped machine swivelled its dish ears toward Luk’s voice. “You have a piston arm that needs a new bearing.”

Luk blinked. “I’ve been here ten seconds.”

“Your right shoulder ticks before you move it.”

Kura laughed. “It hears motor vibration.”

“I hear a great many things,” Zerdata said. “Your voice is the loudest.”

“That’s because it’s the best one.” Luk planted a hand on his chest. “I’m Luk. That’s Isa. This is Kabuto-Horn.”

Kabuto-Horn rolled into the room behind him, its low armored frame balanced on heavy treads. Its twin-pronged horn caught the overhead light. The curved shell protected the torso, and one thick forearm plate lifted in greeting.

“Your machine is built like a freight door,” Zerdata said.

“Thank you,” Luk replied. “It’s a compliment.”

A couple of hours later, the four of them reached the District 4 public practice ring. It was a broad, rubberized court between a salvage depot and a canal garden. Scuff marks and shallow dents covered its surface. A transparent safety barrier surrounded the floor, and old impact pads lined the corners.

Luk had been asking for a match since they left the workshop.

“Just one,” he said. “We keep the power down. You need to see how Zerdata moves when someone is actually coming at him.”

Kura looked at his partner. “Do you want to try?”

Zerdata listened to Kabuto-Horn’s motors. “I would like to know why the heavy one keeps winding up its arm.”

“Kabuto-Horn’s testing the piston.” Luk patted the armored shell. “And now it’s ready.”

The ring’s safety field hummed to life. Kura and Luk stepped behind the boundary line.

“Ready?” Luk called.

Kura raised a hand. “Ready.”

At the signal, Kabuto-Horn surged forward. Its treads rattled across the floor, horn lowered, piston arm cocked for a heavy swing. Zerdata sprang sideways—but the left leg fired a fraction too early. Its foot caught the rubber surface and sent the fox skidding into a turn.

“Zerdata, move!” Kura shouted.

Kabuto-Horn’s horn swept past. The padded tip clipped Zerdata’s shoulder and spun him around. He caught himself on one hand, servos whining.

Luk grinned. “That’s the opening!”

Kabuto-Horn pressed in, swinging its armored forearm. Kura started calling out instructions—duck, turn, move right—but Zerdata was already listening. Its dish ears tilted toward the tightening piston, the uneven tread beat, and the quick scrape of Kabuto-Horn shifting its weight.

“Now!” Kura called.

Zerdata slipped low. The piston crashed into the floor where his head had been, sending a shudder through the ring. Zerdata pivoted and struck the joint behind Kabuto-Horn’s elbow with his resonant claw. The impact rang like a struck bell.

Kabuto-Horn pulled back. Luk’s grin widened. “You can hit harder than that.”

“I can,” Zerdata replied.

A focused sonic pulse burst from Zerdata’s left arm. Kabuto-Horn turned its thick guard plate into the wave. The blast rippled over the armor, making dust jump from the shell, but the heavy frame held its ground.

“Good block!” Luk shouted. “Keep him in front!”

Kabuto-Horn charged again. Zerdata darted to the side, then doubled back as the heavy machine tried to follow. Its treads scraped and pivoted, slower than Zerdata’s springing steps. For a moment, Zerdata had the angle. His claw tapped the exposed shoulder bearing, and the arm jerked out of alignment.

Kura saw the opening and began to shout a plan. “Go behind it, then the leg—no, the other side—”

Zerdata hesitated, trying to follow every command. Kabuto-Horn’s piston swung through the gap. The padded fist struck Zerdata’s torso and knocked him onto his back. The safety field flashed, softening the impact, but the blow still rattled his frame.

“Stop!” Kura called.

Luk raised his hand. Kabuto-Horn backed away at once.

Kura hurried to Zerdata’s side. “Are you all right?”

Zerdata sat up. One shoulder servo trembled, but the diagnostics showed no lasting damage. “I heard the attack. I did not know which instruction to follow.”

Kura looked down, ashamed. “That was my fault. I kept talking over what you could hear.”

“You were trying to help,” Zerdata said.

“I was getting in the way.”

“You can do both.”

Kura checked the shoulder again, although the reading had not changed. He could explain the servo’s hesitation on a diagnostic screen. In the ring, he had barely noticed it before giving three more instructions.

Luk laughed. “Sounds like you two have a lot to learn.”

“From the person who keeps shouting ‘forward’ at a machine with tracks?” Kura said.

“Forward is excellent advice.”

Isa had been watching from the rail, both hands around the strap of her bag. When Kura looked over, she flinched and then gave him a small, bright smile.

“That was amazing,” she said. “The way Zerdata heard the first attack.”

“Thanks.” Kura wiped his hands on a cloth. “You know a lot about AniBot battles?”

“Luk explains them. A lot.”

“I explain it because she asks.”

“You explain it when I’m not asking, too.”

Luk clutched his chest as if wounded. “Betrayed by my own family.”

They left the ring together as evening turned the canopy lights green. At the gate, Luk bumped Kura’s shoulder.

“One day, you and I are going to settle this at the World Cup,” he said. “Grand finals. Kabuto-Horn and Zerdata.”

“Let’s get Zerdata through a full calibration first.”

“I’ll wait.”

“You won’t.”

“Probably not.” Luk offered his fist. Kura bumped it. “Still. Grand finals.”

## 4. What the Notebook Wouldn’t Say

The practice match gave Kura a place to start. It did not give him an explanation.

He spent the next week rebuilding the diagnostic interface around the way Zerdata listened. Instead of sending constant requests through the slate, he used short bursts and waited for the core to answer. He replaced one cracked bearing, balanced the springers, and added a temporary heat sink to the torso. The work was familiar; the signal underneath it was not.

Zerdata improved in small, measurable ways. It could cross the study without stumbling, track a dropped bolt as it bounced across the floor, and tell when a tram was slowing two streets away. It still struggled to connect to modern networks. Its memory banks stayed closed behind the encrypted sectors.

Kura pored over Ty’s notebook. The pages did not tell a story. They showed old wiring plans, resonance curves, notes on heat tolerances, and repeated warnings against forcing a fast connection. One diagram had been torn out. Another contained only a circle around a single phrase: *Generation 0*.

Kura read the words aloud.

Zerdata’s ears snapped upright. The orange glow in its core slot flared, then dimmed. Its hands tightened against the sides of the bench.

“Zerdata?”

“I know that sound.”

“It isn’t a sound. It’s a phrase.”

“Something in me answered it.” The fox’s voice had gone quieter. “I can almost hear a room. Bright lights. A door closing.”

“Do you remember where?”

“No. The memory is behind the lock.”

Kura set the notebook down. The slate showed a spike in the core’s resonance, followed by a sharp fall. He disconnected the test leads and waited for the pulse to return to normal.

“Did it hurt?”

“Not as much as pulling.”

Kura looked at the warning in Ty’s handwriting: *Listen first.* He had spent days treating the locked sectors like a mechanical fault. Maybe the fault was assuming the memory belonged to him to open.

“We can stop here,” he said.

Zerdata’s ears lowered. “I do not want to forget it.”

“Then we won’t force it. We’ll find another way.”

Raya stood in the doorway. She had been carrying a box of old invoices and had heard enough to understand the danger, if not the phrase. Kura held up the notebook.

“Do you remember Dad talking about Generation 0?”

“Not by that name. He told me there were things in the old records people wanted buried.” She set the invoices on a chair. “He wouldn’t explain more. He said that knowing the wrong thing could put a person in danger.”

“Did you ever ask him what he meant?”

“Every time.” She rested a hand on the study doorframe. “He thought leaving us out of it would keep us safe.”

Kura looked at the incomplete diagrams. “It didn’t keep him safe.”

Raya’s face tightened, but she did not argue. “No. It didn’t.”

She crossed the room and put one hand on Kura’s shoulder. “Finding a question is not the same as finding an answer. Promise me you’ll remember that.”

“I promise I’ll be careful.”

“That isn’t the same promise.”

“It’s the one I can make.”

Raya studied him, then nodded. The old study had never felt like part of their home. Now it felt like the first room in a house whose doors had all been shut.

## 5. The First Echo

The next practice session was quieter.

Kura and Luk returned to the public ring after several days of repairs. They agreed to keep the power low and stop as soon as either AniBot showed strain. Isa came along to watch, carrying two drinks from the canal kiosk. She gave Kura one before she could lose her nerve.

“Thanks,” he said.

Her cheeks colored. “You’re welcome.”

Luk clapped his hands. “All right. Rematch. This time, Kabuto-Horn won’t be surprised by the fox’s mysterious ears.”

“I will still hear your arm,” Zerdata said.

“Then I’ll have to be more mysterious.”

The ring’s safety field hummed on. Kabuto-Horn rolled forward, treads steady and low. Zerdata waited until the sound changed, then sprang aside as the horn swept toward him. The heavy machine’s charge carried it past. Zerdata darted in and tapped its back plate with a quick claw strike before bounding away.

“Better!” Kura called.

Luk pivoted Kabuto-Horn around. Its guard plate snapped up just as Zerdata fired a focused sonic pulse. The wave thudded against the armor. Kabuto-Horn pushed through it and swung its piston arm in a wide arc.

Zerdata ducked under the blow. The piston struck the safety-padded floor with a heavy thump. Kabuto-Horn’s treads ground around, trying to pin the smaller machine against the barrier, but Zerdata slipped through the gap beside its shoulder.

“Let it commit!” Kura shouted. “Then go around the—”

The treads began to turn before he finished. Zerdata sprang onto the side of Kabuto-Horn’s shell. Its resonant claw tapped the exposed joint. Kabuto-Horn jolted and twisted, shaking Zerdata loose. The fox landed on his feet and retreated before Luk could bring the horn around.

Luk whistled. “That was clean.”

“Zerdata made it,” Kura said. “I was still trying to call the opening.”

Zerdata’s ears angled toward him. “I heard the treads change.”

Kura nodded. He had been watching the horn.

Kabuto-Horn charged once more. Zerdata sidestepped the horn, but this time Kabuto-Horn anticipated the move and caught his shoulder with the guard plate. The two machines collided and skidded apart. Both stayed upright.

Kura met Luk’s eyes. “Call it?”

“Call it.” Luk raised his hand. The safety field softened to a low glow, and the AniBots settled.

They had traded clean hits, neither gaining enough of an advantage to claim a win. Luk looked pleased anyway. “You gave it a little more room that time.”

“And Zerdata’s learning that I don’t always know what I’m talking about.”

“I knew that already,” Zerdata said.

Kura opened the practice log. The bearings were holding, the temperature was better, and the springers had stopped firing unevenly. He saved those readings, then hesitated over the field marked Handler Notes.

“Come watch Kabuto-Horn train tomorrow,” Luk said. “You can leave the tools here for an hour.”

“I can watch.”

“I know. I want you to tell me what you think I’m doing.”

Kura left the notes field open. “Tomorrow, then.”

Outside, the canal gardens had begun to glow under the evening lamps. Kabuto-Horn rolled beside them, its treads clicking softly. Zerdata walked on the other side of Kura, pausing whenever it found a new sound: water against stone, a cyclist’s chain, the distant hum of the transit loop.

“You think it’s really from Generation 0?” Luk asked.

Kura folded the notebook under his arm. “The chip says G0-004. The notebook says Generation 0. I think they’re connected.”

“Connected to what?”

“I don’t know yet.”

Luk’s grin faded into something more serious. “Then we’ll figure it out.”

“Your World Cup plan now includes research?”

“My plan includes beating you. Research is how I make sure I’m prepared.”

Isa walked close to her brother, listening. “And I’ll help,” she said. “I can keep the notes and look things up.”

Kura looked at her. “That would help.”

She smiled, shy but pleased.

They reached the workshop as the last light drained from the sky. The old study waited upstairs, full of sealed pages and incomplete designs. Ty’s note did not explain why he had chosen this chip for Kura, or how long he had expected it to wait. The encrypted memories inside it remained beyond reach. Ty’s disappearance remained a line in an official file and a silence at the family table.

Kura set the notebook beside the diagnostic slate. Zerdata stood at his shoulder, listening to the tiny pulse inside its own chassis.

“You’re not just a repair project,” Kura said. “I’m going to find out what you are. We’ll do it together.”

“I would like that,” Zerdata said.

On the slate, beneath the unresolved memory warning, a single line appeared in a code Kura had never seen. It vanished before he could copy it. Zerdata’s ears turned toward the empty screen.

For one brief moment, the study filled with the sound of distant machinery—and a door opening somewhere far away.

Then the room was quiet again.
