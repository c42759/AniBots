## 1. Something Worth Bringing Home

The first page held a list of measurements.

Kura had expected something else. A message, perhaps, or a sentence that would make everything after it easier to understand. Instead, his father had recorded the dimensions of a mounting sleeve and crossed out the same figure twice.

Beside the correction was a small sketch of a connector.

Kura knew that connector. He had spent an entire evening trying to replace it in Zerdata’s first body before discovering that its irregular shape was deliberate.

“I thought you’d measured it wrong,” he said softly.

Zerdata looked down at the page.

“It was a very uncomfortable evening.”

Kura smiled, but it did not last. Behind the notebook waited more folders than they could read before dark. The courtyard battle had cost them time, and Vera knew they were somewhere inside the center.

Thorne checked the light beyond the archive windows.

“We should take these back to the ship.”

Kura looked up. “Now?”

“The return passage takes two days. We can work during the journey. Here, we would have to divide our attention between the records and whoever Anode sends next.”

Agon stood near the reading table, its cannon angled toward the floor.

“They will return.”

Kura looked at the open cabinet. Leaving felt wrong after spending so long trying to reach it. Then he thought of the papers becoming trapped here again, or scattered across the floor by another fight.

He closed the notebook carefully.

“We take the whole collection.”

Thorne nodded. “Keep its order. The sequence may matter.”

They packed the bound volumes first, then the drawing cases and tied folders. Cygon recorded the cabinet labels and transfer entries while Luk lined the equipment cases with clean protective sheets. Biggon carried the filled cases into the passage one at a time, setting each down without a scrape.

Kura watched Agon check the corridor.

“Come with us.”

The Primordial turned.

“All three of you,” Kura said. “You knew Dad. You understand parts of this that we don’t. And Zerdata—”

He glanced at his partner.

“I don’t think any of us wants to leave you here.”

Biggon looked toward Cygon. The green lenses remained fixed on the packed records.

“We stayed because Ty might come back,” Cygon said.

Kura did not try to answer that.

Agon crossed the room and rested one hand on a case.

“His work is going with you.”

“Yes.”

“Then so are we.”

They descended from Verdant Plateau by the service path, keeping beneath the trees until Horizon Port came into view. Cygon checked the route ahead where it could. Agon and Holy-Vanguard watched the exposed crossings. There was no sign of the Anode transports.

At the annex, they collected their bedding and remaining supplies. Kura looked once through the office window where he had stood watch that morning. The day had begun with an empty road and three names on a screen. Now those three were waiting outside, helping Luk secure the luggage.

Thorne confirmed their berths on the returning coastal connector and onward passage. The Primordials traveled with them as autonomous AniBots, their places added to the equipment and passenger arrangements before boarding.

Biggon tested the boarding ramp with one foot.

Luk looked at the slight movement beneath it.

“Maybe one at a time.”

“It holds,” Biggon said.

Kabuto-Horn rolled onto the ramp behind it.

Luk put a hand over his eyes.

“That wasn’t an invitation.”

By the time the ship cleared the bay, the archive cases were secured in their cabin and the mountains had become dark shapes behind the stern.

Kura stood at the rail beside Zerdata.

“You found them,” he said.

Zerdata listened to the heavy steps approaching from the companionway.

“We found each other.”

Agon joined them as the last lights of Research Center Ato slipped behind the headland.

## 2. The Work Behind the Frame

The cabin table was too small.

They discovered this within minutes of opening the first case. Thorne needed room for the notebook supports. Cygon needed a clear view of the diagrams. Kura wanted the maintenance slate beside the original measurements, and Luk had put three cups of tea in the only space left.

“No drinks near the papers,” Thorne said.

Luk collected them again.

“The archive rules followed us.”

They worked in turns. Biggon and Kabuto-Horn settled near the secured cases, while Agon watched from the open cabin doorway. Holy-Vanguard remained beside Thorne. Zerdata stood where it could see the pages without leaning across Kura’s hands.

As the ship’s engines settled into their steady rhythm, the collection began to take shape.

There were early studies of Generation 0 circuitry, sketches for compatible bodies, and test records that continued far beyond the engineering transfers at Horizon. Ty had recorded changes in the core’s responses beside changes in the frame, following both through repeated trials.

Other folders contained freight numbers and supplier names.

Kura recognized some from the Citadel files.

“Anode,” he said.

Luk moved closer.

Ty had compared legitimate equipment shipments with missing components, then followed the same serial ranges through unregistered brokers. Several entries carried questions rather than conclusions. Others had dates, quantities, and enough matching details to show that his suspicions had become an investigation.

Thorne read one page twice before placing it aside.

“He was keeping the supply records with the core research.”

“Because people were looking for what he was working on,” Kura said.

“Yes.”

A separate folder bore the Aegis name.

Inside were correspondence copies, equipment allocations, and documents concerning Aegis’s involvement in Research Center Ato. The corporation had supplied research hardware and participated in parts of the center’s work. Its name appeared on access arrangements and technical requests that predated Ty’s disappearance.

Luk looked from one document to the next.

“So Aegis wasn’t just connected through the missing shipments.”

“It had legitimate involvement here too,” Thorne said.

“Which means people around it could know what the center was doing,” Kura replied.

“Some of the work,” Cygon said. “Do not give every name on a delivery form knowledge of every room.”

Kura paused, then nodded.

They separated the ordinary corporate records from pages Ty had marked as suspicious. In the second group, repeated requests concerned old interfaces, pre-standard components, and the origin of particular test assemblies. Ty had underlined questions about where those assemblies were stored.

There was no confession among the pages. No single document explained how much Aegis knew, or who within it was helping Anode. But the overlap was no longer confined to a smuggling route in the Badlands.

“He was working inside a research center that Aegis helped support,” Kura said. “And investigating equipment moving through people connected to Aegis.”

Thorne removed his spectacles.

“A difficult place to decide whom to trust.”

Kura thought of his father working upstairs behind the closed office door. He had remembered those late nights as a child waiting for Ty to finish. Now he could see how much might have been spread across the desk.

He put the Aegis folder beside the Anode records and returned to the engineering notes.

They still had to understand what everyone had wanted badly enough to follow Ty.

## 3. An Attack Without Instructions

The first evening, Thorne and Cygon began comparing the recovered notes with the World Cup telemetry.

Kura watched them work from the opposite side of the table. Outside the cabin window, the sea had darkened until it reflected only the reading lamp.

Cygon brought up the last reliable measurements before the arena instruments reached their limits. Thorne laid a handwritten response curve beside them.

“Here,” the doctor said. “The change in timing.”

“Similar,” Cygon replied. “The magnitude is not.”

They followed the references through two notebooks, then into a folded drawing of the original Echo-Sync assembly. Ty had tested how much variation the frame could accommodate before the supports began resisting the signal they were meant to carry.

Several later notes addressed unrestricted adaptation. Ty had asked whether a body designed around fixed assumptions might prevent an Ancient core from developing responses beyond those assumptions.

Kura read one passage aloud.

“‘A failure to predict the response does not establish a failure of the core.’”

He stopped.

“That sounds like him.”

“It is consistent with his work,” Thorne said.

Zerdata’s ears turned toward the screen as the memory fragments appeared again.

“Does he mention the attack?”

“Not in the pages we have examined.”

“The name?”

Thorne shook his head.

Kura had been looking for a diagram with the answer hidden beneath it: two raised arms, a path through the harmonic channels, a phrase that would explain how the beam had formed.

The pages gave them something harder to use. Ty had understood that the original cores could develop beyond modern expectations. He had been trying to make machinery capable of supporting that development. His questions extended further than his completed tests.

“He knew something extraordinary was possible,” Kura said. “But that doesn’t mean he knew this was possible.”

“Correct,” Cygon replied.

“Then it might not be something he put inside the frame.”

“The frame carried the event. That does not make it the source of every part of it.”

Zerdata looked down at its hands.

“Something I developed?”

“Possibly,” Thorne said. “An ability emerging through your own adaptation. We cannot establish that from a single event, but these notes give us reason to consider it.”

“I do not remember deciding to learn it.”

“We do not yet know how the memories, the berserk state, and the energy release relate to one another,” Cygon said. “We should keep those questions separate until we have evidence connecting them.”

Kura closed the playback before the beam struck.

“Then we still can’t use it as a plan.”

“You did not need it in the courtyard,” Agon said from the doorway.

Zerdata turned.

“That fight was different.”

“You could hear Kura. You could change what you were doing.”

Kura remembered the relief of that single word through the link: *Here.*

He moved the telemetry aside and checked the repaired ear bracket. Zerdata held still while he tested the alignment.

“We’ll keep trying to understand it,” Kura said. “But I want you with me when we fight.”

“So do I.”

The cabin shifted gently beneath them. Thorne held one hand against the open notebook until the movement passed.

“For tonight,” he said, “I suggest we stop before fatigue begins supplying answers the papers do not.”

This time, Kura closed the book without an argument.

## 4. The Seven They Carried

The next morning, Biggon found Kura on deck before breakfast.

He had gone outside with a cup of tea and no notebook. The ship was crossing open water beneath a low bank of cloud, its wake stretching pale behind them.

Biggon settled beside the rail with enough space left for people to pass.

“You are awake early.”

“So are you.”

“I like the sound.”

Kura listened. Water along the hull. The deep engine vibration. A loose fastening ticking somewhere above the deck.

“Zerdata would tell me which fastening that is.”

“Vulpes listened before it could move.”

“Were the others like that?”

Biggon looked toward the companionway.

“Ask us together.”

After breakfast, the seven survivors became names across the cabin table.

Vulpes Zerda. Panthera Uncia. Acinonyx Jubatus. Orcinus Orca. Varanus Komodoensis. Harpia Harpyja. Octopus Cyanea.

Kura had seen the list in the records from Zero. Hearing Agon speak each name changed it.

“They were spread across different tests,” the Primordial said. “Different affinities. Different ways of answering. When the decommissioning order came, we could not reach everyone.”

Biggon’s hands rested against its knees.

“We carried who we could.”

Zerdata stood very still.

“I remember the signals ending.”

Cygon looked toward it.

“That is enough for now.”

Agon described the seven in the terms of someone who had known them: the patient responses, the rapid ones that arrived before a test had finished, the patterns that seemed to follow several conversations at once. Kura recognized echoes of the personalities cataloged in the old research, but Agon remembered interruptions, preferences, and small acts of persistence the catalog had never recorded.

It did not offer a reliable map of where all seven were now. Separation, long periods without contact, and incomplete information had left gaps even the Primordials could not fill.

Kura did not press for certainty they did not have.

Instead, he unfolded the unfinished chassis designs.

“These were for them.”

Cygon drew the nearest page closer. “At least some of them.”

There were different sensor arrangements, support structures, and interfaces. One assembly allowed several channels to operate together. Another gave unusual space to a resonance array. Many dimensions remained blank.

Ty had written questions beside them rather than forcing every design toward the same answer.

Luk followed a line across one drawing.

“He wasn’t making seven versions of Zerdata.”

“No,” Kura said. “He was trying to work out what each one needed.”

Agon looked at the page for a long time before speaking.

“That was why I trusted him with Vulpes.”

Kura lifted his eyes.

“He listened when the core did not answer the way he expected. He changed the equipment before deciding the response was wrong. And he asked what Vulpes would experience through it.”

Zerdata’s ears lowered, then rose again.

“He wanted to know what I could hear.”

“Yes.”

“I remember that.”

Kura pictured his father beside a silent cartridge, waiting for a response that might arrive as a pulse on an instrument rather than a voice.

He had done the same thing at the workshop, without knowing how much of the work had begun before him.

“These designs could still help,” he said.

Thorne nodded. “With care. Some were never tested.”

“I know. But they don’t stop mattering because Dad couldn’t finish them.”

Agon rested its hand beside the drawing, keeping the old paper flat as the ship rolled.

“No,” it said. “They do not.”

## 5. The Last Entries

They found the final research entries that afternoon.

They were inside a narrow folder beneath a set of component studies. Ty had dated the pages, but the handwriting grew less even toward the end. Some lines had been added after the main entry, squeezed along the margin.

The early pages described damaged Generation 0 hardware recovered from the Vault 7 events.

Thorne adjusted the lamp.

“He had physical material.”

Cygon examined a cross-section beside the notes.

“Original substrate. The interface arrangement fits.”

Ty had studied the wreckage of an Ancient unit destroyed during the incident. Among the remains were damaged AniChip components that allowed him to examine parts of the original architecture unavailable in modern commercial systems.

Kura looked toward Agon.

“Did you know?”

“He told us he was studying old material. He did not show us every part of his investigation.”

The next sheet listed observations that even Cygon read slowly. A damaged assembly exposed internal relationships the surviving chips could not reveal without being dismantled.

“He could study this because it was already broken,” Kura said.

Thorne nodded.

Zerdata took a small step nearer the table.

“Could it be repaired?”

The doctor waited before answering.

“The records describe a destroyed core. They give us no reason to believe its life could be restored.”

“But the information—”

“These are observations of its construction,” Cygon said gently. “They are not a copy of the one who lived there.”

Biggon lowered its head.

No one turned the page immediately.

When Kura did, the research language changed.

There were fewer measurements. More references to questions being asked, people pressing for access, and equipment inquiries that no longer seemed ordinary. Ty had written about the growing risk of keeping Vulpes near his work.

Kura read faster until Thorne touched the table beside his hand.

“Slowly.”

He stopped at the final entry.

For several seconds, he could not make himself read it aloud.

Then he did.

> They are getting closer. I can no longer guarantee Vulpes’s safety while the chip remains with me.
>
> I will keep only the remains of the Generation 0 unit destroyed during the Vault 7 incident. Its architecture may still provide answers, but it is no longer what they are searching for.
>
> Vulpes must be kept somewhere they will never expect.
>
> I have made my decision.

The engines continued beneath the silence.

Kura stared at the last line. He had found his father’s decisions scattered through measurements, wiring paths, and crossed-out drawings. This one reached straight into the room upstairs at home.

The closed office. The covered chassis. The chip resting beside the note.

“He moved Zerdata away from the research,” he said.

Luk sat beside him without speaking.

“He knew people were following the work. So he left the damaged material with himself and put Vulpes somewhere else.”

Thorne’s face tightened.

“That is what the entry says.”

“With us.”

Zerdata looked at Kura.

The pages did not tell them what happened afterward. They did not name a final meeting, identify everyone pursuing Ty, or say where he had gone. The decision was there. The road beyond it remained missing.

Kura put his hand over his mouth and looked away from the table.

Luk stayed beside him.

Nobody asked him to continue reading.

## 6. What His Father Left

Later, Kura took a copy of the entry onto the sheltered deck.

Zerdata followed him. Through the glass doors behind them, Thorne and Cygon were securing the original papers. Agon remained beside the table, looking at the place where the final entry had been.

Kura rested his elbows on the rail.

“I thought he’d left me a machine.”

Zerdata stood beside him.

“I thought I was supposed to make it work because he couldn’t finish showing me how. Then I thought he wanted me to find out what happened to him.”

He looked down at the copied words.

“Maybe he wanted those things too. But he was trying to keep you alive.”

“I was with him when it became dangerous,” Zerdata said.

Kura turned at once.

“You didn’t make it dangerous.”

“He kept me.”

“Because people were looking for you. That was their choice.”

Zerdata’s fingers tightened against the rail, then released it.

Kura remembered the first morning at the workbench: the frantic voice asking for Ato, the hurt when he tried to pull at a locked memory, the surprise when he stopped.

His father could not have known those moments. He could leave a frame, a core, and a hope. He could not build the days that followed.

“He trusted me to look after you,” Kura said.

Zerdata’s ears turned toward him.

“Before I’d done anything. Before the fights, or Jax’s lessons, or the V2. I was twelve when he disappeared.”

Kura let out an uneven breath.

“I wish he’d told me.”

“Would you have understood?”

He considered that honestly.

“Not all of it.”

The sea moved darkly beneath the rail.

“I’m not sure I understand all of it now.”

Zerdata waited.

Kura lowered the device.

“But I understand that you weren’t a thing he wanted someone to own.”

“No.”

“And he couldn’t decide who we’d become to each other.”

Zerdata looked back through the doors, toward the notebook.

“He gave me the name. You asked whether I wanted to keep it.”

Kura remembered.

“You stayed when the first body broke,” Zerdata continued. “You built the second. You stopped the tests when I could not do what you wanted.”

“I wanted you safe.”

“I know that now.”

For a while, they watched the wake without speaking.

Then Kura opened a video call home.

Raya answered from the kitchen. Isabell was still at the workshop and joined her a moment later, wiping her hands on a clean cloth.

Kura waited until his mother had sat down before showing her the entry.

She read it in silence.

When she finished, she kept looking at the screen.

“All those nights,” she said. “I knew he was frightened of something.”

“He was trying to protect Vulpes.”

Raya nodded slowly.

“Did you find anything about what happened to him?”

Kura shook his head.

The disappointment crossed her face before she could hide it. He was grateful when she did not try to hide it for long.

“We found why he left the chip,” he said. “We still haven’t found where he went.”

“All right,” she whispered.

Zerdata moved closer to the camera.

“I am sorry I cannot remember more.”

Raya looked up.

“You don’t owe us memories you haven’t got.”

Kura watched its shoulders ease.

He told them about the unfinished designs and the connection between Aegis and the research center. Thorne joined the call to explain what the documents established and what remained uncertain. When Agon appeared behind him, Raya stopped speaking.

“You knew Ty.”

“Yes,” Agon said.

“Then come home with them. Please.”

“We are.”

She nodded, pressing her lips together.

After the others moved back inside, Isabell remained beside the screen.

“How are you?” she asked Kura.

He looked at the copied entry again.

“I don’t know yet.”

“That’s all right.”

He gave a small, tired smile.

“How’s the workshop?”

“Organized. Busy. Waiting for its mechanic.”

“In that order?”

“Mostly.”

Raya reached for the device. “And the mechanic should eat dinner before reading another page.”

Luk called from the cabin doorway.

“I’ve been saying that for an hour.”

“Then bring him inside,” she said.

Kura put the copied entry away and followed his friend.

## 7. The One Without a Name

They returned to the damaged Ancient’s records on the second evening.

This time, Kura let Thorne lead.

The collection contained drawings and material studies, but no intact designation tying the wreckage to a name. Labels identified assemblies by reference number. Several fragments had been too badly damaged for Ty to place with certainty.

Agon studied the surviving outlines.

“Can you recognize it?” Kura asked.

“Not from this.”

Biggon moved closer, then shook its head.

Cygon compared a diagram with its own remembered architecture. “There are features here I have never examined directly.”

Thorne marked the relevant passage on a separate sheet.

“Which explains why Ty kept studying it. A destroyed assembly could expose structures that remained inaccessible in a functioning core.”

Kura looked at the measurements that had occupied his father during the last days recorded here.

“Even after Vulpes was gone, he still had something valuable.”

“Potentially,” Thorne said.

“Enough for Magnus to want?” Luk asked.

“Perhaps. We must not mistake a reason he might have had for evidence of what he did.”

Kura nodded. He wrote the question beside the others instead of drawing a line beneath it.

Where were the remains now? What had Ty learned from them? Who knew he possessed them? What happened after the last entry?

The papers did not answer.

They made copies of the research observations and packed the originals back in order. Cygon kept the technical index with Thorne’s notes. The Aegis correspondence remained beside the Anode investigation, their overlap marked carefully rather than treated as a completed case.

Agon helped close the final drawing case.

“He wanted us to have bodies we could live in,” it said.

Kura looked at Zerdata’s repaired ear, then at the unfinished designs waiting inside the case.

“We can keep working on that.”

“And Magnus?”

Kura rested his hands on the lid.

“He’s still looking for you. For all of you.”

Agon nodded.

“Then we face that together.”

On the final morning of the passage, Kura woke to Luk fastening the luggage straps.

The cases were stacked beside the cabin door. Biggon waited patiently while Kabuto-Horn insisted on taking one of the heavier loads. Cygon checked that every copied file could be opened. Thorne sat with his eyes closed, his spectacles folded neatly in his lap.

Zerdata stood by the window.

“We’re near the terminal,” it said.

Kura joined it. Beyond the glass, the coast had emerged through the morning haze.

He had left home looking for instructions his father might have written. He was returning with unfinished research, evidence of a danger Ty had recognized, and the decision that had put Vulpes in his care.

He still did not know how Anima Limit Breaker worked.

He still did not know what had happened to Ty.

But the note upstairs would never read quite the same way again.

Kura lifted his bag and checked the notebook case one last time.

“Ready?” Luk asked.

Zerdata turned from the window. Behind it stood Agon, Biggon, and Cygon, waiting to leave the ship with them.

“Ready,” Kura said.

Together, they carried Ty’s work toward the door.
