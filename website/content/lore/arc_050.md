## 1. Something to Tell Your Mom?

By the time Kura finished Master Jax’s training, the Ato Family Workshop had learned how to make room for a new routine. Salvaged actuators waited in clean trays beside customer repairs. Jax’s careful notes sat open near Kura’s tablet, and Zerdata stood ready on the reinforced test platform, its rebuilt joints moving far more smoothly than they had after the Highlands.

Isabell had started visiting more often.

Sometimes she brought a question about Swan-Grace’s sensors. Sometimes she came with a small repair for Kura to check. Sometimes she arrived with no excuse beyond a paper bag of snacks and the hope that he would be free to talk for a little while. She would sit at the end of the bench, sorting screws or holding a flashlight while he worked.

Kura had started expecting her visits. He told himself it was because she was helpful. Then he caught himself glancing at the shop door whenever the bell rang.

One afternoon, Isabell had just left after helping him compare Zerdata’s balance readings when Raya came in from the office, carrying the parts ledger.

“I’m seeing Isabell more often in the shop lately,” she said, her voice carefully casual. “Is there something you want to tell your mom?”

Kura nearly dropped his wrench. “What? No.”

Raya looked at him over the ledger. “No?”

“She’s a friend. She’s helping with Swan-Grace’s scans. And she knows a lot about the sensor systems.”

“Of course.” Raya turned a page, hiding a smile. “I was only asking.”

Kura bent over Zerdata’s open shoulder panel, suddenly very interested in a perfectly ordinary connector. “There’s nothing to tell.”

“All right, Kura.”

Raya returned to the office. Kura waited until she was gone before letting out the breath he had been holding.

There was nothing to tell. He was sure of that.

Mostly.

The qualifying season began two weeks later at the District 5 sports complex. The World Cup was still months away, but the qualifier circuit had already drawn Handlers from across Neo Metropolis. Corporate academy teams warmed up beside independent garages. Families filled the stands, and the wide arena screens displayed match schedules, safety notices, and the names of the next competitors.

Luk was practically vibrating with excitement. “This is it. First step toward the finals.”

“First step is check-in,” Kura said, glancing at the registration kiosk.

“You can check in while I get excited.”

Isabell and Swan-Grace had come to support them. She wore her tournament jacket over her usual outfit, the white rabbit charm on her communicator bouncing as she hurried to keep up. When she saw Kura, her smile came quickly and stayed a little longer than it used to.

“Good luck,” she said. “Both of you.”

“Thanks,” Kura said. He found himself smiling back before he could think about it.

Anda joined them near the warm-up area with Ancient-Wyrm at his side. He had already checked the match board and was studying the listed opponents.

“You’re seeded lower than you should be,” he said to Kura.

“I’ll survive.”

“That remains to be seen.” Anda’s gaze shifted to Zerdata. “The machine’s movement is cleaner than it was in District 9.”

“Kura’s been working on it,” Isabell said, with a note of pride.

Kura looked at her. Isabell quickly turned her attention to Swan-Grace’s ribbon tie.

The first matches began.

## 2. The Qualifier Circuit

Kura’s opening opponent was a Handler from District 3 whose AniBot, Rivet-Ram, carried a reinforced head and a pair of short battering horns. The machine charged as soon as the referee gave the signal.

“Zerdata, angle right!”

Rivet-Ram’s first rush cut across the arena. Zerdata sidestepped, but the opponent pivoted fast and clipped its hip. Zerdata staggered; the rebuilt gyro steadied the frame before it could fall.

Kura watched the other machine’s feet. “It needs a full step to turn. Keep moving.”

Zerdata lured Rivet-Ram toward the arena boundary, then cut sharply across its front. The heavier bot tried to turn and struck the padded barrier shoulder-first. Before it could recover, Zerdata rushed in and drove a short punch into the side of its knee joint. Rivet-Ram dropped to one leg.

Its Handler called for a last charge. Zerdata ducked beneath the horns, caught the machine around the waist, and twisted. Rivet-Ram rolled onto its back, its legs kicking once before the referee called the match.

Kura had won.

The victory was narrow. His shoulder still tensed when Zerdata was hit, and he studied every diagnostic line after the match. But the rebuilt joints had held. The actuator did not overheat, and the gyroscope correction had worked exactly as he hoped.

“That was a good first match,” Isabell said when he came back from the arena.

“You mean a good first win?” Luk asked, stepping up after his own bout. Kabuto-Horn had just defeated a nimble opponent by catching its leap and flipping it over one shoulder. “Because I had a good first win.”

“You nearly fell over when Kabuto-Horn landed,” Isabell said.

“That was part of the strategy.”

The qualifying rounds stretched across several days. Kura and Zerdata faced opponents with newer parts, stronger frames, and faster response systems. Kura won by studying how each AniBot moved and making the arena work for them: a quick feint to draw an overconfident charge, a low sweep to unbalance a tall opponent, a sharp retreat that coaxed a ranged machine into wasting its best shot against the barrier.

The wins brought attention. Some people praised Kura’s timing. Others focused on Zerdata’s old design and wondered how long it could keep pace. A few assumed that Kura’s success came from some trick hidden in the machine.

Soren Vance watched from the upper rows.

He was easy to notice. His Aegis team jacket was pristine, his posture composed, and Tigris-Fury stood behind him with the stillness of a predator waiting in tall grass. Its orange-and-charcoal armor looked freshly tuned. Every movement of its limbs was smooth and quiet, with no visible hitch or wasted motion.

After Kura’s second win, Soren came down to the edge of the warm-up floor.

“That machine is older than you are,” he said.

“Probably,” Kura replied.

“Yet you keep making it win.” Soren looked past Kura to Zerdata. “What exactly is inside it?”

“A lot of repairs.”

“That’s not an answer.”

“It’s the one you’re getting.”

Soren’s expression barely changed. “People with the right connections would pay a great deal to know what makes that chassis different.”

Kura felt his shoulders stiffen. “I’m here to qualify.”

“So am I.” Soren turned away. Tigris-Fury followed, its optics fixed on Zerdata until the Handler disappeared into the corridor.

“I don’t like him,” Luk said.

“You don’t like anyone who thinks he’s better than you,” Anda replied.

“I like people who prove it in a match.”

The next afternoon, Kura met Dr. Aris Thorne near the equipment inspection tables. The elderly scientist had come to observe the independent competitors. He was short and slight, dressed for travel, with a small paladin-like AniBot standing beside him.

“You are Kura Ato?” Dr. Thorne asked.

“Yes, sir.”

Thorne looked at Zerdata. His attention lingered on the head assembly, then on the places where the old chassis joined to Kura’s newer repairs.

“May I ask where you acquired this machine?”

“It belonged to my father.”

For a moment, Thorne looked as if he might ask another question. Instead, his expression softened into a careful smile. “Your father had an eye for unusual engineering.”

“You knew him?”

“I knew of his work.” Thorne paused. “Keep your records. Every repair, every change. Some machines carry more history than their owners realize.”

Before Kura could ask what he meant, a tournament official called Thorne away. The scientist gave Zerdata one last, searching look and left with Holy-Vanguard at his side.

Kura watched him go.

“That was strange,” Luk said.

“He recognized something,” Kura replied.

“Or he likes old machines,” Isabell offered. “Either way, he seemed kind.”

Kura opened his diagnostic tablet and recorded the brief conversation alongside the match results. He had learned from Jax that small details could matter later.

## 3. The Match Against Soren

By the time Kura reached the upper bracket, the qualifier venue had grown louder and more crowded. Anda had advanced through his own side of the draw. Luk was still in contention. Isabell stood with them at the gate to the main arena, hands clasped around her communicator.

Kura’s next match was against Soren.

Tigris-Fury entered the arena to a wave of cheers from the Aegis section. It moved with quick, economical steps, its tail balancing each turn. Zerdata walked in opposite it, older and more visibly repaired, but steady.

“You made it this far,” Soren said as the handlers took their marks. “That’s more than most expected.”

“I don’t need you to expect anything.”

“Good. You’ll need your concentration.”

The referee gave the signal.

Kura began with movement. “Zerdata, keep your distance!”

Zerdata darted sideways as Tigris-Fury sprang forward. The first claw strike cut through empty air. Zerdata slipped behind it and landed a quick punch against the ribs, then leaped back before Tigris-Fury could turn.

Kura had studied the footage. He knew Tigris-Fury was faster, so he tried to make that speed work against it. Zerdata feinted toward the center, then drew the opponent into a wide turn. Tigris-Fury’s claws scraped the floor as it overcommitted. Zerdata ducked under the next swipe and struck its shoulder joint.

For several seconds, Kura’s plan held. Zerdata avoided the heaviest blows and answered with fast, precise attacks. The crowd began to respond to the unlikely exchange.

Then Tigris-Fury changed its rhythm.

It stopped chasing Zerdata’s movements and cut off the escape paths instead. A sudden kick clipped Zerdata’s ankle. Before it could recover, Tigris-Fury slammed into its side and drove it across the arena.

“Zerdata!”

The old AniBot rolled, came up on one knee, and raised its guard. Its damaged shoulder trembled under the strain.

Soren’s voice carried across the field. “You can keep rebuilding it. But Magnus would be very interested in what you inherited from Ty Ato.”

Kura froze for a fraction of a second.

“Don’t listen to him!” Luk shouted from the sideline.

“Focus on the match,” Isabell called. “Kura, you can do this!”

Kura looked at Zerdata, then back at Tigris-Fury. Soren had said his father’s name deliberately. He wanted Kura distracted, and the realization steadied him.

“Zerdata, low and left!”

Tigris-Fury lunged. Zerdata dropped beneath the claws and drove a punch into its forward knee. The impact made the joint buckle. For the first time, Tigris-Fury lost its balance.

Kura saw the opening. “Push through!”

Zerdata struck the side of Tigris-Fury’s torso and shoved it back two steps. The audience rose with a roar.

Soren’s expression hardened. “Enough.”

Tigris-Fury’s actuators surged. It recovered and rushed in with a blur of claw strikes. Zerdata dodged the first, twisted away from the second, and caught the third on its forearm. The blow bent the old armor inward. Zerdata staggered.

“Back away!” Kura called.

Tigris-Fury did not give it room. It struck Zerdata’s damaged shoulder, then swept its legs from beneath it. Zerdata hit the floor hard. Before it could rise, Tigris-Fury seized its arm and wrenched it sideways. The assembly tore free with a harsh metallic crack.

Isabell gasped. Luk gripped the barrier rail.

Kura’s hand tightened around the control link. “Zerdata, roll away!”

Zerdata pushed with its remaining arm and rolled clear. It rose unsteadily, one side of its frame exposed. Its cyan optics flickered, but held.

Tigris-Fury advanced. Kura called for another evasive maneuver. Zerdata tried to pivot, but one damaged leg caught under its frame. Tigris-Fury swept in and struck the leg assembly. Metal folded. Zerdata dropped to one knee.

Soren leaned forward. “You should tell me what that chip is.”

“No.”

“Then you’ll lose it without knowing why it matters.”

Kura’s stomach turned cold. “Zerdata, get up!”

The machine pushed against the arena floor. Its head lifted, one optic dim and sparking. For a moment, it managed to stand. Kura could see its frame shaking through the control feed.

Tigris-Fury drove forward. Its clawed strike slammed into Zerdata’s upper frame and sent it crashing down. The old chassis skidded across the floor and stopped near the boundary line.

“Zerdata!”

The referee stepped in. Zerdata’s remaining hand moved once, trying to push itself up, then fell still. Its optics dimmed.

The referee raised a hand. “Match to Soren Vance. AniBot unable to continue.”

The crowd’s roar turned distant in Kura’s ears.

He ran onto the arena floor and dropped beside Zerdata. The torn arm lay several feet away. One leg assembly had been crushed beyond use; the head casing was cracked, one optic dark.

“Zerdata, can you hear me?”

There was a faint crackle in the ear link. “Kura... I am sorry.”

“Don’t be. Don’t say that.”

Tournament staff began to approach with a recovery platform. Kura placed a hand against Zerdata’s chest plate, careful not to press the damaged seams.

Soren walked past them with Tigris-Fury. “You should have told me what it was.”

Kura looked up. “You should have fought the match you came here to fight.”

Soren paused, then continued toward the exit.

## 4. What Was Left

Kura returned to the workshop with Zerdata secured in the back of the transport. He barely spoke during the ride. Raya was waiting at the door when he arrived, having watched the result through the tournament stream.

She did not ask about the score.

She helped him carry the damaged chassis inside, then gave him room to set Zerdata on the main workbench. The broken parts lay beneath a clean cloth. The workshop seemed too bright, too ordinary, around the silent machine.

Luk arrived a little later. Anda came with him. Isabell entered last, Swan-Grace walking quietly beside her. She stopped when she saw the torn arm and bowed head casing.

“Kura...”

He did not answer at first. He was staring at the notebook he had taken from his father’s office, its pages open beside the diagnostic tablet.

“I thought I had made it strong enough,” he said. “I used Jax’s techniques. I replaced what I could. I knew there were limits, but I thought if I watched the load and kept moving—”

Raya placed one hand on his shoulder. “You did everything you could with what you had.”

“It still wasn’t enough.”

Isabell stepped closer. She wanted to reach for him, to tell him that he had not failed, that Zerdata was still here. Instead, she waited until he looked up.

“You stayed with him,” she said. “You listened to him. You gave him every chance you could.”

Kura’s face tightened. “I couldn’t stop it.”

“I know.” Her voice was quiet, but she did not look away. “You don’t have to pretend it doesn’t hurt.”

For the first time since the match, Kura’s eyes left the notebook. He looked at her—really looked—and the steadiness in her expression gave him a place to rest his thoughts.

He reached out. Isabell took his hand.

Neither of them said anything for a while.

Later, when his friends had gone and Raya had made tea, Kura opened Ty’s notebooks again. He searched for every page that mentioned Zerdata’s acoustic synchronization, every sketch of the frame, every note about power distribution and harmonic response.

His father’s design had never been meant to fit neatly into a standard chassis. The frame had been built around the unusual Vulpes Zerda core, and its systems spoke to one another through acoustic patterns older than the modern control standards. Kura could not simply bolt on stronger parts and expect them to work.

He needed a new body.

Not a replacement machine. Not a copy of a modern tournament frame. A chassis designed to preserve Ty’s synchronization principles while using stronger materials, cleaner power delivery, and modern manufacturing tolerances.

Kura called Master Jax the next morning.

“I need your help building something new,” Kura said.

“You need help, or you need a place to start?”

Kura looked at Zerdata’s damaged frame. “A place to start.”

“Good. Bring every notebook you have.”

## 5. Building a Second Body

The reconstruction took weeks. The workshop became a meeting point for designs and arguments. Kura worked with Ty’s notes spread across one table and modern chassis specifications on another. Jax challenged every shortcut. Raya kept the customer work moving and helped Kura track the costs of each component. Zerdata’s core remained protected in a temporary diagnostic cradle, its consciousness dormant while Kura and Jax prepared the frame it would inhabit.

Isabell returned often. She helped test sensor layouts and brought Swan-Grace’s scanning gear to map the frame’s balance points. She asked Kura questions that made him explain his decisions aloud, which often revealed a problem he had not noticed.

She also brought food. Sometimes she stayed late enough to help Raya close the shop.

One evening, Kura caught himself hoping she would come through the door. When the bell rang and it was only a customer collecting a repaired unit, he felt foolishly disappointed.

Raya noticed. She said nothing, but her smile carried all the teasing she had not spoken.

The first frame design failed. Its armor was strong but too heavy; the acoustic response arrived late, and the core’s signals distorted under motion. Kura and Jax stripped it down and began again.

The second design held together in the test rig, but the power conduits overheated when Zerdata’s core was connected. Kura stayed up through the night with the wiring plans. Isabell sat across from him, tracing the signal path with a stylus.

“This branch is too direct,” she said. “It’s sending the pulse back before the first harmonic settles.”

Kura checked her line, then leaned closer. “You’re right. If we route it through the buffer, it’ll hold the timing.”

Their shoulders touched. Isabell’s face warmed, but she remained focused on the diagram. Kura adjusted the circuit, then looked at her.

“I’m glad you’re here.”

Isabell’s stylus stopped. “I’m glad I can help.”

She wanted to say more. She did not. Instead, she smiled and returned to the diagram.

The third design began to feel right. Its frame kept Zerdata’s familiar silhouette, but the internal supports were new. Kura used modern composite armor over reinforced joints, balanced the power distribution around the core, and rebuilt the sensor array to work with the machine’s acoustic perception. Jax helped him align the actuators by hand, checking each movement instead of trusting the factory tolerances alone.

When the chassis was ready, Kura lowered Zerdata’s core into its housing.

The connection sequence was quiet. No flare of light, no dramatic surge—only the soft pulse of the diagnostic cradle and the rising hum of the new frame. Kura watched the readings, one hand poised above the cutoff switch.

“Core synchronization stable,” he murmured. “Acoustic channel clear. Power delivery holding.”

The new AniBot’s fingers flexed.

Kura leaned closer. “Zerdata?”

The optics opened, cyan and clear.

“Kura?”

His breath caught. “I’m here.”

Zerdata sat up slowly. The new frame moved with a smoothness the old chassis had never managed, though its balance shifted as it adjusted to the unfamiliar body. Kura stayed by the platform, talking it through each movement.

“How do you feel?”

“Different,” Zerdata said. “The sound of the workshop is clearer. My movements feel... connected.”

Kura’s eyes shone. “We built it for you.”

Jax crossed his arms. “You built it. I kept you from making it worse.”

“That too,” Kura said.

The chassis was not a miracle. Its output still had limits, and Kura would need to test every system carefully. But it held the core’s unusual architecture without forcing it into a standard pattern. Zerdata could move, turn, and respond without the old strain.

They named the new body Zerdata V2.

## 6. The Last-Chance Bracket

Kura’s loss to Soren had removed him from the main qualification route. The tournament officials confirmed it in a short notice: he could still enter the World Cup through the independent last-chance bracket, but he would have to earn the place. There would be no automatic return and no special allowance because of Zerdata’s damage.

Kura accepted the entry form.

The last-chance event took place a month later at a smaller arena. The crowd was thinner, the production less polished, but the competitors were no less determined. Luk had qualified through the main bracket and arrived with Isabell to watch. Anda came too, carrying a tablet full of match recordings and pretending he had not come specifically to support Kura.

Before Kura’s first match, Isabell found him by the staging area.

“You’ve tested every movement?” she asked.

“Three times.”

“And the power flow?”

“Stable.”

“And you’re not going to take unnecessary risks just to prove the new chassis is strong?”

Kura smiled. “I’ll try not to.”

“Kura.”

“I promise.”

She searched his face to make sure he meant it. “I’ll be right there.”

He felt a quiet warmth settle in his chest. “I know.”

The opening match tested Zerdata V2 against a quick, low-profile AniBot that attacked with short bursts and retreated before Kura could close the distance. The new frame responded well, but Kura had to learn its reach and turning speed in real time. He called for a broad step to intercept; Zerdata moved farther than he expected and nearly crossed its own line.

“Shorter stride,” Kura corrected. “Then pivot.”

Zerdata adjusted. The next time the opponent rushed in, Zerdata caught its shoulder, turned with the momentum, and sent it sliding across the floor. The match ended with a clean sweep and a firm pin.

Kura advanced.

The second match was harder. His opponent used a heavy shield bot that tried to crowd Zerdata against the boundary. The new chassis could take a hit, but Kura refused to turn the match into a contest of raw force. He drew the shield bot into a charge, sidestepped, and struck the hinge behind its shield. The machine lost its guard for a moment. Zerdata used the opening to slip past and push it down from behind.

At the end of the day, Kura stood one match away from the last-chance final.

The night before, he could not sleep. He sat in the workshop with Zerdata, running through movement checks under the dim bench lamps. Isabell arrived with two cups of tea and found him recalibrating the left wrist for the fourth time.

“It’s fine,” she said.

“I know.”

“You’re still adjusting it.”

“I know that too.”

She placed the tea beside him. “You don’t have to make everything perfect before you’re allowed to trust it.”

Kura glanced at her. “That sounds like something Raya would say.”

“Maybe I’ve been listening to her.”

They sat together in the quiet workshop. Isabell wanted to tell him that she was proud of him—not just for building a new chassis, but for standing back up after losing Zerdata. She wanted to tell him she had been thinking about him every day since the Highlands, and that being beside him had become important to her.

Instead, she lifted her cup. “To tomorrow.”

Kura tapped his cup against hers. “To tomorrow.”

## 7. A Place Earned

The final match of the last-chance event was against a Handler whose AniBot used twin arm blades and quick, side-to-side rushes. Zerdata V2 met the first charge with a raised forearm. The strike rang across the arena, sending a tremor through its new armor.

“Don’t trade hits,” Kura called. “Keep the center.”

Zerdata stepped back, turned, and let the bladed bot pass. It struck at the air, pivoted, and came again from the opposite side. Zerdata dipped under the blades and hit its midsection with a short punch. The opponent rocked back but stayed upright.

The Handler called for a spinning attack. The twin blades swept out in a fast arc. Zerdata ducked, but one blade scraped its shoulder plate, leaving a bright line in the composite. Kura’s stomach tightened. The image of the old chassis flashed in his mind.

He forced himself to watch the whole fight, not the damage.

“Zerdata, listen for the right actuator!”

Zerdata tilted its head. The opponent’s next rush came with a faint hitch in its right side. It was small, nearly hidden by the fast movement, but the sound gave it away.

“Now!”

Zerdata moved into the gap. It caught the attacker’s wrist, turned under the arm, and used its shoulder to throw the bladed bot off balance. The machine stumbled. Zerdata struck its back with a controlled shove, sending it to one knee, then knocked one blade from its hand with a sharp kick.

The opponent tried to rise. Zerdata held its forearm across the machine’s shoulder and kept it down without striking again.

The referee stepped between them and called the match.

Kura had earned the final qualifying place.

For a moment he did not move. The crowd was cheering. Luk was shouting his name. Anda gave a restrained nod. Isabell hurried to the edge of the arena, bright-eyed and smiling.

Kura looked at Zerdata first. The new frame stood steady, its optics glowing.

“We did it,” Kura said.

“We did,” Zerdata answered.

Then Kura turned and saw Isabell. She was waiting just beyond the barrier. He walked over, still catching his breath.

“You made it,” she said.

“We made it.”

Her smile softened. “I’m proud of you.”

He had heard cheers from the stands, and congratulations from the other Handlers. Isabell’s words reached him differently. He found himself wanting to stay beside her a little longer, even while Luk called him back for a photograph.

“Thanks, Isa,” he said.

She blushed at the nickname, but she did not look away.

Across the venue, Soren watched a replay on a screen. Zerdata V2’s lines were modern, the movement smooth, the acoustic response hidden in its timing. He saw only the new silhouette and the qualifier result—not the extent of Kura’s reconstruction or the way the old design lived inside it.

The official list updated on the arena display. Kura Ato and Zerdata V2 were registered for the World Cup.

Luk slung an arm around Kura’s shoulders. “Now you’re really going to meet me in the finals.”

“We both have to get there first,” Kura said.

“We will.”

Isabell stood on Kura’s other side, close enough that their sleeves touched. For once, neither of them stepped away.

Kura looked from his friends to Zerdata. The loss to Soren had taken the body his father built, and the rebuild had asked more of him than any repair before it. But Zerdata was still here. The machine had a second body, Kura had earned his place, and a new road opened ahead.

He was beginning to understand that he wanted Isabell beside him on that road, too.

He did not know what to call the feeling yet. He only knew that when she smiled at him, the noise of the arena seemed to soften around them.

The World Cup awaited.
