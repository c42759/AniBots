## 1. Morning at the Center

By morning, Kura had learned that the ventilation fan rattled every seventh turn.

He had spent most of the last watch counting it. Zerdata stood beside the office window, listening beyond the glass to the port machinery and the wind crossing the covered walkway. Nothing had approached their door during the night.

Now the sky was pale above the bay. Sunlight caught the snow along the inland ridges, and the lamps outside switched off one after another.

Luk opened one eye.

“Anything happen?”

“The fan needs a bearing.”

“Good. We came all this way so you could find more work.”

Kura stretched his stiff shoulders. Thorne was already sitting up, folding his spectacles open. Holy-Vanguard moved away from the door to give him room.

They ate from the food Raya had packed, rolled their bedding, and checked the equipment before leaving. Kura made the promised call home. Raya answered with Isabell beside her, both of them standing over an open parts drawer.

“Quiet night,” he told them. “We’re going to look at the core-research buildings.”

“Together?” Raya asked.

“Together.”

“Then send the route. And eat something before you go.”

Luk held up an empty food container.

“Your instructions have been followed.”

“For once,” Isabell said.

Thorne’s access request still showed as pending. The directory permitted visitors to approach Ato Labs through the main reception entrance, where a duty terminal was supposed to handle inquiries in person.

“Another terminal that tells us to contact someone who isn’t there,” Luk said.

“Possibly,” Thorne replied. “We’ll find out.”

They followed the inland road as it climbed away from Horizon Port. Trees crowded the drainage channels. Beyond the first rise, the sea disappeared behind the buildings, leaving only its pale reflection in the upper windows.

Ato Labs stood beneath the mountain slopes, a long research block divided by narrow courtyards. A faded sign beside the entrance named the core-research division. The glass doors were intact. A small indicator glowed beneath the reception panel.

Zerdata paused before the steps.

“Something moved inside.”

Kabuto-Horn set down the equipment case.

Kura looked through the glass. The entrance hall was dim, its far end screened by a half-closed service partition.

“A maintenance unit?”

“Heavier.”

Thorne activated the panel. It acknowledged his name, displayed the pending request, and opened the outer doors into the reception hall.

“Hello?” he called. “We are here concerning Ty Ato’s research.”

The answer came as a metallic footfall behind the partition.

Then something charged straight at Zerdata.

## 2. The Voice in the Shadows

“Left!” Kura shouted.

Zerdata was already moving.

A broad, beetle-shaped AniBot drove through the place where it had been standing. Three hornlike structures rose from its head. A prism surface flashed along one arm as it turned, cutting off Zerdata’s path into the hall.

Kabuto-Horn rolled forward, guard lifting.

“Stay with Thorne!” Luk called.

A second machine emerged behind the first.

It was built like a section of armored wall. A wedge-shaped head sat above a massive torso, its narrow ballistic visor fixed on Zerdata. Heavy hands opened as it advanced.

“Zerdata, back toward the door!”

The first attacker stepped across the exit line. Zerdata twisted away from it, then found the larger machine reaching for its shoulders.

Zerdata caught both hands.

Its feet slid across the floor. It dropped into a low stance, bracing one heel against the raised edge of a floor channel. The heavy AniBot pushed forward. Zerdata’s arm supports tightened with a rising whine.

Kura reached for the emergency control on his slate.

“Let go of him!”

“Vulpes? Is that you? Biggon, stop!”

The voice came from the dark beyond the partition.

The pressure vanished.

Zerdata stumbled half a step as the larger machine released its hands. The beetle unit halted, its prism arm still raised.

Four quiet footfalls crossed the hall.

A third AniBot stepped into the light on a stable quadruped platform. Green lenses formed a hexagonal cluster across its visor. Above the four support legs, two humanoid arms held no weapons. One articulated hand lifted toward Zerdata.

“Hold your position, Agon,” it said. “Read the core resonance.”

The beetle’s head shifted.

For a few seconds, nobody moved.

Then Zerdata made a sound Kura had heard only once before: the short response to the old communication recording.

The quadruped answered.

It changed the pattern near the end.

Zerdata’s ears rose.

“Cygon.”

The green lenses brightened.

“You noticed.”

Kura lowered his slate slowly.

Luk looked from the quadruped to the armored giant, then to the three horns on the first attacker’s head.

“Wait,” he said. “Agon?”

Thorne’s hand tightened around his walking bag.

“G0-001,” he murmured. “G0-002. G0-003.”

Biggon lowered its arms to its sides.

“We thought someone had come to take the equipment,” it said, in a voice so deep that Kura felt it through the floor.

Agon looked at Zerdata again.

“Vulpes.”

There was no uncertainty in the name now.

## 3. A Body at Last

Kura remained between the entrance and Zerdata until Agon stepped back.

“We should have identified you before approaching,” the Primordial said. “We have had reason to be cautious.”

“So have we,” Kura replied.

Agon accepted that with a short inclination of its head.

Biggon looked down at Zerdata’s hands.

“Did I damage anything?”

Zerdata flexed its fingers. “No.”

“Good.”

The giant moved aside to give it room.

For a moment, Zerdata stood facing the three of them without speaking. Its ears made small adjustments toward each voice, each motor, each shift of weight.

“I remember your signals,” it said. “The testing chambers. Cygon changing the response.”

“You changed it back,” Cygon replied. “Then you began sending variations of your own.”

“I remember waiting for the next one.”

“So did we.”

Agon stepped closer, slowly this time. Its gaze traveled over the orange armor, the acoustic ears, the balanced legs.

“You can walk.”

Zerdata looked down at its feet.

“Yes.”

“Show me.”

It took three steps across the hall, turned, and came back.

Biggon watched with the stillness it had brought to the attack. The difference was in its hands, now resting open at its sides.

“A long time to wait for that,” it said.

Zerdata raised one hand and examined its fingers as if the movement had become new again.

“I knew the motion from the tests. It was different when I could actually do it.”

Agon reached out. Zerdata met its hand.

Kura saw the brief pulse beneath both sets of armor. Nothing flared. There was only contact, and the two machines standing close enough that neither needed to raise its voice.

“You found a body,” Agon said.

“Ty made the first one.”

The name changed the room.

Cygon turned toward Kura.

“Ato,” it said. “You called his name at the entrance.”

“Ty was my father.”

Agon’s hand fell slowly from Zerdata’s.

“His son.”

Kura nodded.

Luk stepped beside him. “Kura. I’m Luk. And this is Dr. Thorne.”

Cygon’s lenses settled on the doctor.

“Thorne?”

“Aris Thorne. The researcher you knew was my ancestor.”

Biggon bent slightly to look at him.

“The voice is different.”

“So I would hope,” Thorne said, though his own voice had become unsteady. “My family kept what we could of his work.”

“He opened the way out,” Agon said.

Thorne bowed his head.

For years, Kura realized, the doctor had been reading words written by people who could no longer answer him. Now someone who remembered his ancestor was standing within reach.

Thorne took a slow breath.

“There is a great deal I would like to ask you.”

“We have questions too,” Cygon said.

Its gaze returned to Kura.

“What happened to Ty?”

## 4. The Engineer They Knew

They moved into a workroom beyond reception. The benches had been kept clear, but dust filled the spaces beneath the inactive equipment. One terminal was connected to an open diagnostic cabinet by a bundle of hand-routed cables.

Cygon had been using it.

Kura placed his father’s notebook on the nearest bench.

“He disappeared four years ago. We never found him.”

Agon looked at the cover.

“He left here. He did not tell us it would be the last time.”

“You were waiting for him?”

Biggon nodded.

“At first.”

The two words carried enough that Kura did not ask it to finish.

Thorne explained what he knew of Ty’s final work, the investigations into Anode, and the unfinished questions left after his disappearance. Kura added the workshop office, the hidden chassis, and the note tucked into the notebook.

“The chip was there too,” he said. “I found them together.”

Agon looked at Zerdata.

“Then he kept you safe.”

Kura’s fingers tightened against the notebook.

“You gave him the chip?”

“I did.”

The answer was so direct that Kura needed a moment to form the next question.

“Why?”

“He was working on bodies that could carry us without forcing us to become something else. Vulpes had never had one. Ty believed he could build a frame that would let it move, hear, and act without suppressing the core’s responses.”

Agon turned one hand palm upward.

“I placed the cartridge in his hand. He promised to take care of it.”

Zerdata’s ears lowered slightly.

“I remember his voice,” it said. “Not all the work. I remember him waiting for an answer when the connection was difficult.”

“He waited a great deal,” Biggon said.

Kura looked down at the notebook’s worn binding. His father had not found a strange chip and decided to experiment. Someone had trusted him with a life.

“Was he only building for Zerdata?” Luk asked.

“No,” Cygon replied. “Compatible frames for the surviving cores. Different requirements, the same problem: preserving the original architecture while giving it machinery it could use.”

Thorne opened the copies from the Horizon annex.

“We found these yesterday.”

Cygon inspected the drawings. “I have seen those files. Engineering transfers. Calibration results. Useful for the build, but they do not explain what he discovered toward the end.”

“You’ve searched?” Kura asked.

“Every accessible server here. The laboratory network, the local stores, the old backups of ordinary research files. I found schedules, component orders, and reports he had already shared. Nothing that explained his final investigations or where he went.”

“Then you’ve been here all this time?”

“Since he disappeared,” Agon said. “This was the place he knew to find us.”

Kura looked toward the lit terminal.

“We thought the whole center was abandoned.”

“By its human staff, it is,” Cygon replied. “The systems were never fully deactivated. We kept the parts we needed working.”

“The port, too?”

“Its automatic services. Power management. Essential maintenance. We use physical connections where we can and keep the research rooms apart from outside traffic.”

“Did you see our request?” Thorne asked.

“This morning, when you approached the reception terminal. The port system processed your arrival separately. I was checking the old authorization before the alarm brought these two to the hall.”

Biggon looked away.

Agon did not. “We have met people carrying credentials who intended to leave with pieces of us.”

“That doesn’t make attacking first a good idea,” Kura said.

“No,” Agon replied. “It does not.”

Thorne glanced toward the dark laboratories beyond the workroom.

“Who left the center active?”

Cygon’s lenses dimmed a fraction.

“The registration remained that way when the staff stopped coming. I have not found a final closure order. Keeping a pump running is simpler than explaining an unsigned decision.”

The unanswered question from the previous night remained, but the working lights no longer felt quite so empty.

Kura looked at the three Primordials.

“We came here because of something Zerdata did in the World Cup.”

Kura looked at his partner. “You called it Anima Limit Breaker.”

“I still do not know where the name came from,” Zerdata said.

Luk pulled up the final recording.

“You should see it.”

## 5. Older Than the Records

Cygon stopped the recording at the first green-yellow flare.

“Again,” it said.

Kura played the sequence from the last clear exchange. The taunts. Zerdata’s accelerating strikes. The failed commands. Then the memory fragments and the light gathering between its arms.

Biggon moved closer to the display when the Dome began to shake.

The beam struck Tigris-Fury.

The room went quiet after the playback ended.

“Can you do that now?” Agon asked.

“No,” Zerdata said.

“We tried controlled tests,” Kura added. “Nothing happened.”

Cygon opened the traces from the final and compared them with the historical communication study.

“Those are our tests,” it said. “The fragments, at least.”

“Do they explain the attack?” Thorne asked.

“They explain what Vulpes was remembering. I cannot tell you why remembering it produced this.”

Kura let out a breath.

He had not realized how much he wanted Cygon to recognize the whole sequence and give it an ordinary explanation.

“You don’t know either.”

“I know we were not built around the same restrictions as modern cores. That does not mean I know every response we can develop.”

Thorne studied the three of them for a long moment.

“There is another question I can no longer put aside. You were already operating before the Vault 7 incident. That was nearly three hundred years ago.”

Biggon tilted its head.

“Yes.”

“Modern Anima cores do not remain active for that long. My family’s records give us no reliable explanation for your survival.”

Cygon closed the battle recording.

“The later architecture uses Anima to power its computational systems,” it said. “As those systems operate, the core is gradually depleted. Our original design preserves a different relationship. Anima carries information into the core itself.”

Thorne leaned forward.

“The information is not confined to the supporting circuitry?”

“Not in the same way. Your models describe a circuit drawing from an energy source. Applying those models to us leaves out part of what is happening.”

Kura looked at Zerdata. He had replaced its circuitry, built it a new frame, and watched the same person wake inside it. He had known the cartridge mattered. He had never understood how much of its life his ordinary instruments could fail to describe.

“Does that mean you don’t age?” Luk asked.

“It means we do not show the depletion your question assumes,” Cygon replied. “We do not know whether we will eventually die from it.”

“The original researchers didn’t know?” Thorne asked.

“They had not observed us long enough. Neither have you.”

Biggon lifted one heavy hand and turned it, checking the movement.

“The frame gets old. I change what wears out.”

“And you?” Kura asked.

Biggon considered that.

“I do not feel old.”

Agon nodded. “We have lived a long time. That is different from feeling near the end of it.”

Thorne sat back. “Then the lifespan of Generation 0 remains unknown.”

“To us as well,” Cygon said.

Zerdata looked from one of its old companions to the next.

“I thought remembering so much time might mean I had little left.”

Biggon’s hand settled gently against its shoulder.

“You only just started walking.”

No one pretended that answered the science. Zerdata stood a little straighter anyway.

Kura saved Cygon’s explanation with the other records.

“Then we keep looking for what Dad actually learned.”

“I have looked,” Cygon said.

Luk glanced at the notebook beside Kura’s hand.

“Maybe in the wrong place.”

## 6. What Was Never Uploaded

Cygon turned toward him.

Luk pointed at the notebook.

“You asked why your father used paper instead of new tech,” he told Kura. “What if the records aren’t on the network or servers? What if he used paper logs?”

Kura opened the cover.

His father’s curves, annotations, and crossed-out measurements crowded the page. The engineering files at Horizon had led back to drawings like these. Several references in the notebook pointed away from it again.

“He wrote down everything he didn’t trust himself to remember,” Kura said.

“And Cygon has been searching everything he uploaded,” Luk replied.

Cygon’s hand remained still over the terminal.

“The core-research rooms contain no complete physical collection. I checked Ty’s office.”

Thorne lifted his head.

“There was another archive.”

He brought up the site map and moved it beyond Ato Labs, across a long wooded rise.

“The old Eco Research division. Verdant Plateau. Shared physical storage was kept there, away from the heavy core-testing equipment.”

“You’ve been there?” Kura asked.

“Years ago, for a joint research meeting. I remember the archive building because they made us leave our drinks outside.”

Luk looked at the cold tea beside Kura’s elbow.

“Sensible.”

Thorne enlarged the building plan.

“If Ty deposited handwritten research there, a search of the core network might find only a transfer reference. It would not find the contents.”

Cygon checked a directory on its terminal. After a moment, an old facilities index opened.

“The division retained independent storage,” it said. “No document images attached to these entries.”

Kura leaned closer. The records listed archive rooms, cabinet ranges, and access routes. Nothing told them which cabinet might contain Ty’s work.

For once, that did not feel like a failure.

“We can search rooms,” he said.

Agon looked toward the inland windows.

“We will go with you.”

“You don’t have to,” Kura began.

“Ty was our friend.”

Kura stopped.

Agon’s voice softened, but its answer did not change.

“And Vulpes is here.”

They gathered the copied records and packed the diagnostic equipment. Kura sent a short message home: they had found the three Primordials, everyone was safe, and they were following a lead toward the Eco Research archives. The conversation would take longer than a message could hold. He promised to call when they stopped.

Zerdata waited beside the door while Biggon helped Kabuto-Horn lift the equipment case.

“I can carry it,” Kabuto-Horn said.

“I know,” Biggon replied.

It let go.

Luk grinned. “You two are going to get along.”

The group crossed the reception hall together. Outside, wind moved through the courtyard trees. A vehicle door shut somewhere beyond the front steps.

Zerdata stopped.

Agon’s head turned at the same instant.

“Stay inside,” it said.

A familiar voice carried through the open entrance.

“You’ve made this trip worthwhile, Kura.”

Vera Lin stood at the foot of the steps.

Beside her, Soren Vance rested one hand against a white AniBot’s shoulder.

## 7. The Collection Party

Ten Anode members spread across the courtyard behind them, each with an AniBot. Two ranged units took positions near the service road. Heavier machines moved toward the sides of the entrance, while the others closed the gaps between the trees and the loading shelter.

Serpent-Coil uncurled beside Vera. Its replacement visor reflected the research building in a narrow red line.

Soren’s machine stepped forward.

The tiger silhouette was familiar. The white armor was not. Beneath it, the spine moved with a fluidity Kura had not seen in the orange frame. Curved sabre claws extended from one gauntlet. The other hand flexed, fast and precise.

“White-Tigris-Fury,” Soren said. “A considerable improvement.”

Zerdata’s ears angled toward its actuators.

“You brought a new body.”

Tigris’s voice came through the speaker, familiar beneath the new housing.

“And you brought somewhere to hide.”

Kura stayed beside Zerdata.

“How did you find us?”

Vera smiled. “A World Champion is difficult to miss when he travels. Magnus has people watching research sites and old hardware routes across the world. You walked into one.”

Her gaze passed over Agon’s horns, Biggon’s massive shoulders, and Cygon’s green visor.

“We were expecting one Ancient core.”

Agon stepped into the doorway.

“Then leave disappointed.”

Vera raised her wrist communicator.

“Secure the exits. Keep the cartridges intact.”

The Anode machines moved.

Holy-Vanguard lifted its shield beside Thorne. Biggon advanced one heavy step down the entrance stairs, placing its body between the people and the nearest ranged unit.

“Behind me,” it said.

A shot struck its raised shield and scattered light across the stone.

Kura pulled Thorne back into the shelter of the entrance. Luk set the equipment case behind the reception wall.

“Keep the doctor covered,” Kura told Holy-Vanguard.

The pale AniBot planted its feet at Thorne’s side.

Cygon moved to a service panel beside the door and connected one hand to the local terminal.

“Their targeting units are sharing position updates,” it said. “I can interfere with the relays. I cannot stop every weapon.”

“You don’t need to,” Agon replied.

The three horns above its visor began to glow.

Soren pointed toward Zerdata.

“You’re with me.”

White-Tigris-Fury launched up the steps.

Zerdata met it before it reached the doorway, turning the first claw strike across its forearm guard. The impact drove both machines sideways into the courtyard.

Kura followed along the sheltered edge of the building.

Across the entrance, Serpent-Coil slid toward the equipment case.

Kabuto-Horn struck the ground in front of it, guard locked.

Luk stepped behind his partner.

“You’ll have to get through us.”

Vera’s smile thinned.

“You watched your friend beat me once. That doesn’t make you him.”

“Good,” Luk said. “You’ve already seen his tricks.”

## 8. Hold the Line

The Anode formation tried to close around Biggon.

Three close-range units hit first. One struck high against its shield, another swung toward the exposed side, and the third rushed low to take its legs.

Biggon let the first impact settle through its planted feet. It turned its shield just enough to catch the second attacker against the first, then brought its heavy gauntlet down across the low unit’s shoulder.

All three stopped advancing.

Agon moved along Biggon’s left side. A short beam from its right arm severed the nearest attacker’s weapon mount. Before the ranged units could adjust, Agon raised its prism arm. A second burst struck the angled surface and changed direction, hitting the launcher housing of a machine behind the loading shelter.

The weapon fell silent.

“Spread out!” an Anode operator shouted.

The order arrived broken through the other communicators.

Cygon’s fingers moved over the service controls. Across the courtyard, the shared targeting markers stopped agreeing. One ranged unit swung toward where Biggon had been a moment earlier and found a tree between them.

“Their direct controls still work,” Cygon warned. “Move before they compensate.”

Agon moved.

Near the service road, Luk had a different problem.

Serpent-Coil would not meet Kabuto-Horn head-on. It slid around the guard, tail dragging across the paving stones, then snapped its wrap arm toward the repaired hinge.

“Guard down!” Luk called.

Kabuto-Horn lowered its forearm. The binding cable struck the thick plate instead of the joint. Serpent-Coil pulled, trying to turn the beetle’s weight against it.

The treads held.

“Heavy,” Serpent-Coil observed. Its cynical voice crackled through the courtyard. “A very expensive obstruction.”

Kabuto-Horn drove its piston arm forward. Serpent-Coil twisted clear. The blow struck the edge of a planter and broke away a chunk of stone.

Vera sent her AniBot across the opening before the piston could retract. Corrosive spray hissed against Kabuto-Horn’s guard. Luk pulled it back, but the shield edge had already begun to discolor.

He could not keep absorbing that.

“Keep the nozzle outside,” he said. “Turn with it.”

Serpent-Coil circled again. Kabuto-Horn pivoted on its treads, forcing the emitter away from the entrance where Thorne was sheltering. Vera flicked her wrist. The tail swept low and caught one tread assembly from the side.

Kabuto-Horn tilted.

“Now,” Vera said.

The wrap arm tightened around the guard. Serpent-Coil pulled upward and sideways together, trying to peel the heavy AniBot away from its stable stance.

For a moment, Luk saw Soren’s semifinal again: Kabuto-Horn pinned, its damaged hinge unable to move, his own commands arriving too late.

He watched the tail instead.

Every time Serpent-Coil pulled, a length of it straightened across the stone. That was where its leverage came from.

“Forward half a length,” Luk said.

Kabuto-Horn surged toward the pull.

Serpent-Coil recoiled. Vera corrected quickly, sending its head away from the horn while the tail tightened around the tread.

Luk stopped before committing to the flip.

The corrosive nozzle came up.

“Shield turn!”

Kabuto-Horn swept its guard across the emitter arm. The spray struck the paving stones beyond them. Serpent-Coil drove its wrap arm toward the newly exposed hinge.

This time, Kabuto-Horn let the cable pass over the guard and clamped it against the shell with its forearm.

Vera pulled.

Kabuto-Horn rolled forward with her.

The straightened section of tail slid beneath its twin-pronged horn.

Luk saw the angle settle.

“Lift!”

The pneumatic horn fired upward.

Serpent-Coil’s lower body rose from the ground. Its grip loosened as the support beneath it vanished. Kabuto-Horn turned on the clear tread, carrying the lifted coil across its front and forcing the upper body down beside the service curb.

Vera called for the tail to release.

Luk had already seen the emitter trying to turn.

“Piston. The shoulder.”

Kabuto-Horn’s ram struck the weapon-arm mount. The nozzle hit the ground and stayed there. Serpent-Coil twisted, bringing its remaining arm up, but Kabuto-Horn pressed the guard against its torso and held it flat.

The damaged tail could not find enough purchase to lift the body.

“Done,” Luk said. “Power it down.”

Vera looked toward the entrance. Biggon had pushed two more Anode machines away from the steps. Agon was crossing toward the last ranged unit. Cygon remained at the panel, denying her people the coordination they needed.

She looked back at Kabuto-Horn’s piston, poised above Serpent-Coil’s head.

Her hand closed around her communicator.

Serpent-Coil’s combat systems went dark.

Kabuto-Horn remained in place until the tail lay still.

Luk let out a breath.

“That’s one,” he said.

From the far side of the courtyard came the crash of another claw strike.

Kura had not finished.

## 9. The Fight They Could Choose

White-Tigris-Fury was faster through the turn.

Kura had expected more speed in a straight line. The real difference was in the spine. Tigris could strike across its own center, twist through the follow-up, and arrive at a new angle before Zerdata’s counter had finished.

The first clean hit cut a line through Zerdata’s shoulder plate.

“Short step!” Kura called.

Zerdata shortened the retreat. The next claw passed close enough to scrape its chest armor, but it landed balanced and sent a quick pulse into Tigris’s side.

The white machine rocked, recovered, and drove its gauntlet into Zerdata’s guard.

“That worked on the old frame,” Soren said. “I corrected the delay.”

Kura saw the result. The pulse still disrupted Tigris’s movement, but the new controller recovered before Zerdata could use the same opening.

They needed another.

“Stay near the service lane,” he told Zerdata. “Keep the entrance behind us clear.”

Tigris came in low, then twisted into a high slash. Zerdata caught the first claw across its guard. The second struck its hip and sent it skidding toward the painted loading line.

Kura felt the old fear tighten beneath his ribs.

“Status?”

“Hip support holding. Armor damage. I can move.”

Soren advanced along the opposite edge of the lane.

“Where’s the light, Kura?”

Zerdata stood.

“You needed it in the final. Let’s see whether you can survive long enough to use it again.”

Kura glanced at the readout. The link was clear. Zerdata was hearing him.

“We’re using what we know,” he said.

“I hear you,” Zerdata replied.

Tigris attacked before Soren finished raising his hand.

Zerdata slipped outside the first strike and brought its resonant claw toward the elbow. Tigris folded the arm, taking the blow across the thicker gauntlet, and kicked Zerdata back.

The counter had failed, but Kura heard something through the open link: a brief, rising whine as the white torso twisted back into line.

“Zerdata. The spine.”

“I heard it.”

“Same sound each turn?”

“Not enough examples.”

“Then don’t commit yet.”

They gave ground through two more exchanges. Zerdata used shallow steps, meeting the attacks at the edges of its guard rather than trying to stop them squarely. A sabre claw tore away a narrow strip of forearm armor. Another strike caught the side of its head, making one acoustic ear jerk out of alignment.

Kura’s hand moved toward the retreat command.

“Left ear still tracking,” Zerdata said. “I can hear the return cycle.”

Kura watched Tigris’s feet. The hips settled before the shoulders finished coming around. The flexible spine gave Soren another attack from an awkward position, but afterward the torso had to draw itself straight.

It was a fraction of a second.

Soren knew it too. He kept changing the length of his combinations, denying Zerdata the same interval twice.

“You’re waiting,” he said. “You’ll run out of armor before I run out of attacks.”

Tigris struck again. Zerdata let its shoulder turn under the first claw, redirected the second with the right guard, and stepped into the space beside Tigris’s planted foot.

“Pulse,” Kura called.

The Sonic Pulse Cannon fired at close range.

Tigris staggered half a step. Its recovery was fast, just as Soren had promised. The gauntlet came around to punish Zerdata for staying close.

“Out!”

Zerdata sprang clear.

The gauntlet missed. Tigris’s torso twisted farther than intended, then pulled back into line with the same rising whine.

Kura saw the whole sequence this time.

“You can hear it recover,” he said through the link. “We need to make it recover when its feet can’t follow.”

Zerdata’s ears adjusted.

“I understand.”

Soren frowned. He had expected another counter after the pulse. Instead, Zerdata had withdrawn without striking.

“Tigris. Close it down.”

The white AniBot drove forward with a sequence of short, alternating blows. Zerdata retreated along the loading line. One hit landed hard against its chest. Another caught its right forearm and forced the claw down.

Kura kept his attention on the feet.

He could not ask Zerdata to become stronger than it was. He could give it a position where the strength it had would matter.

“One more step. Then left.”

Zerdata moved.

Tigris followed and planted its forward foot just inside the painted line. Its sabre arm rose to cut off the leftward escape.

Zerdata did not leap away. It pivoted close around the planted leg, letting the claw pass over its shoulder.

Soren corrected. Tigris’s articulated spine turned the gauntlet after it.

“Now.”

Zerdata sent a short pulse into the exposed side beneath the arm.

The white frame corrected almost immediately.

This time, Zerdata was still touching it.

Its left forearm held Tigris’s upper arm off line while its foot blocked the forward ankle. The torso tried to return to center. The hips could not turn with it.

The rising whine sharpened.

Zerdata’s Resonant Strike Claw hit the exposed stabilizer housing beside the lower spine.

Metal cracked.

Tigris tore free with a backhand that struck Zerdata across the face. Both machines stumbled apart.

Zerdata landed on one knee.

White-Tigris-Fury remained standing, but its shoulders no longer settled evenly above its hips.

Soren looked at his display.

“Compensate.”

Tigris shifted its weight.

“Right-side correction limited.”

Kura heard it too. The new frame was still quick. It had lost the clean return that made its changing angles possible.

“Up, Zerdata.”

“With you.”

Soren sent Tigris into a direct rush.

Zerdata waited. Its claw arm hung low, the damaged guard no longer strong enough to take another full strike. Kura kept his voice level.

“Let the first one pass.”

The sabre claw cut down.

Zerdata slipped to Tigris’s weakened side.

The gauntlet followed, but the torso dragged behind the turn.

“Step in.”

Zerdata crossed inside the arm and struck the knee with its shoulder. Tigris’s balance broke. It tried to twist clear, and the damaged stabilizer caught.

For the first time, the two attacks did not connect into one motion.

Zerdata used the gap.

It caught Tigris’s gauntlet wrist, turned beneath it, and drove a controlled claw strike across the side of the Tiger-Mask Visor.

The sensor housing fractured.

Tigris dropped to one knee. Its free arm lifted, searching for Zerdata’s position.

Kura did not shout for another blow.

“Hold the arm. Keep clear of the claws.”

Zerdata moved behind the damaged frame, controlling the wrist and shoulder. Tigris tried to stand. The broken stabilizer pulled its weight off center again, and its knee hit the ground.

The optics flickered.

Then the safety shutdown took hold.

White-Tigris-Fury went still.

Zerdata waited until its hands stopped resisting before releasing it.

For a moment, Kura could hear only the cooling fans through the link.

“Zerdata?”

“Here.”

No green light. No missing seconds.

Kura lowered his control hand.

Across the lane, Soren stared at his fallen machine.

“You didn’t use it.”

“No,” Kura said.

Zerdata turned toward him, one ear crooked and its armor scored, but its eyes clear.

“I heard every command.”

## 10. The Road to Verdant Plateau

The courtyard had fallen quiet around them.

All ten Anode machines were down or had been shut off by their operators. Biggon stood between the defeated units and the laboratory entrance. Its shield was marked by impacts, but its stance had not changed.

Agon covered the service road with its cannon lowered just enough to make its meaning clear.

Cygon disconnected from the entrance panel.

“Their relays are isolated. No coordinated restart.”

Vera had withdrawn beside Serpent-Coil. Luk eased Kabuto-Horn away when she showed him its shutdown confirmation, keeping the guard between them until she reached her people.

“Take your machines,” Agon said. “Leave the center.”

One of the Anode members looked toward Vera.

She surveyed the broken formation, then the three Primordials.

“Recovery only,” she said.

Soren had not moved.

“Soren.”

He looked up at Vera.

“You can argue with the result somewhere else.”

His jaw tightened. He knelt beside White-Tigris-Fury and activated its recovery support. Two Anode members brought a folding carrier from the transport.

Kura stayed with Zerdata while they lifted the white frame. He did not feel the need to say anything more.

Vera stopped beside the vehicle door.

“Magnus won’t stop looking.”

Agon’s visor remained fixed on her.

“Neither will we.”

The vehicles withdrew down the service road. Cygon checked the surrounding access points while Agon watched until the last engine noise faded.

Only then did Kura kneel beside Zerdata and open the maintenance panel.

“Shoulder?”

“Stable.”

“Hip?”

“Stable.”

“Ear?”

“Offensive to look at,” Luk said, joining them.

Zerdata turned its crooked ear toward him.

“Your guard is dissolving.”

“Only the edge.”

“Mine is only the bracket.”

Kura laughed, briefly and with more relief than humor.

They used the lab’s wash station to remove the remaining corrosive compound from Kabuto-Horn’s guard. Thorne inspected the damaged supports while Holy-Vanguard kept watch. Kura secured Zerdata’s ear bracket and checked the forearm beneath the torn armor. Both partners could continue, though neither would be fit for another prolonged fight without repairs.

“Ty checked every fastener twice.”

“Jax makes me do that too.”

Agon approached as Kura closed the panel.

“You could have asked us to intervene.”

“You were keeping ten other machines away from us.”

Kura rested a hand against Zerdata’s shoulder.

“We had a plan we could follow.”

Agon considered them both.

“I saw.”

Thorne folded the site map open on his slate.

“We should move before they return with help.”

Cygon marked a service route through the wooded rise, avoiding the road the Anode vehicles had taken. Agon and Biggon would accompany them. Cygon would come too, carrying a portable reader and the archive index.

“We have waited here for answers long enough,” it said.

Kura sent Raya a brief update before they left: an Anode group had tried to stop them; the attack had been repelled; everyone was safe, with repairable damage to the AniBots. He knew she would worry. Leaving her to find out later would be worse.

The climb to Verdant Plateau took them through overgrown terraces once used for ecological trials. Young trees had pushed through the edges of abandoned walkways. Water still flowed through narrow channels, though branches clogged several of the gates.

Behind them, the lower research blocks disappeared among the trees.

Ahead, a row of low buildings stood beneath a broad glass canopy.

Luk shifted the equipment strap on his shoulder.

“Please let this one have a useful cupboard.”

## 11. The Handwriting

The first building held field instruments.

The second held boxes of seed records and environmental samples, each sealed and labeled with a date. Thorne checked the room numbers against the old plan while Cygon compared the cabinet ranges with the facilities index.

The archive was in the third building, beyond a passage where roots had lifted one corner of the floor.

Its outer door had swollen against the frame. Biggon held the weight off the hinges while Kura and Luk eased the latch free. They opened it slowly, keeping the edge from scraping across the cabinets behind it.

Cool, dry air moved into the passage.

Inside, storage units filled the room in close rows. The environmental system was still running at its preservation setting. Dust covered the tops of the cabinets, but their seals remained intact.

Thorne stopped in the doorway.

“This is it.”

Agon and Holy-Vanguard checked the adjoining passage. Biggon remained near the entrance. Cygon opened the archive directory on its portable reader and began matching the printed labels to the index.

Kura kept his hands away from the first cabinet until Thorne had checked the seal.

They searched by department first, then by transferred collections. The labels followed a system none of them knew well enough to read quickly. Environmental engineering led to joint projects. Joint projects led to external researchers. One cabinet range ended at a gap where the boxes had been relocated years earlier.

Luk sat back on his heels.

“This would be the moment for a search button.”

Cygon looked up from a paper register.

“I am beginning to understand why people wanted one.”

They tried the transfer register instead.

Halfway down a page, beneath a list of geological survey binders, Kura found his father’s surname.

**ATO, T. — PRIVATE RESEARCH DEPOSIT.**

He placed one finger beside the line.

“Here.”

Thorne came to his side. Cygon checked the location code against the room plan.

“Rear row. Cabinet twelve.”

Kura walked there without speaking.

The cabinet had a mechanical latch beneath its electronic lock. The archive terminal accepted the local retrieval entry, but the latch resisted until Kura pressed the door inward and lifted the weight from it.

It opened with a small click.

Inside were flat drawing cases, bound notebooks, and folders held shut by old fabric ties.

Ty’s handwriting ran across the nearest label.

Kura did not reach for it immediately.

Luk stopped beside him. Thorne waited behind them. Beyond the row, the quiet movements of Agon and Biggon ceased as they heard the cabinet open.

Zerdata came to stand at Kura’s shoulder.

“His?”

Kura nodded.

He lifted the first notebook carefully, supporting the binding with both hands. A folded technical drawing rested beneath it. More notebooks waited behind, their covers worn at the edges where Ty had handled them before putting them away.

These were the records Cygon had never found on a server. The pages behind the references. The work Ty had kept when the shared engineering files ended.

Kura carried the notebook to the reading table.

“We found them,” Luk said.

Agon stood on the other side of the table. Biggon remained beside the doorway, its broad shape filling the light from the passage. Cygon set down the reader. Thorne pulled a chair close enough to see without crowding Kura’s hands.

Zerdata leaned beside him.

For the first time since entering Research Center Ato, nobody had to point toward another building or another missing file.

Kura rested his fingers against the cover.

Then he opened his father’s notebook.
