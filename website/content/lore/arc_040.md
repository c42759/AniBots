## 1. The Parts That Wouldn’t Hold

When Kura returned from District 9, the workshop was busier than he had left it. Raya had filled the repair board with customer jobs, and two delivery units waited beside the counter with their casings open. Still, the first thing he did was clear a space on the central bench for Zerdata.

The highlands fight had left its marks. Zerdata’s shoulder actuator dragged under load. Fine cracks had formed around one hip assembly, and the old power couplings heated too quickly whenever Kura asked for a hard burst of movement. The machine could walk, turn, and perform basic drills, but every repair Kura made seemed to uncover another weak point.

He replaced a bearing. The next day, the joint casing flexed around it. He reinforced the casing. Then the old actuator began to stutter. A modern replacement fit the socket, but its control profile fought Zerdata’s older frame and drained power unevenly.

“It’s not that the parts are impossible to find,” Kura said, reading the diagnostics again. “It’s that the parts that fit are worn out, and the new ones don’t talk to your system properly.”

Zerdata sat on the workbench, legs dangling over the edge. “The replacement actuator responds to input. It does not respond well.”

“That’s a polite way of saying it’s terrible.”

“I was attempting to be encouraging.”

Kura smiled, but the scan still bothered him. He had spent years learning from manuals, watching his father work, and taking apart whatever reached the shop. Since finding Ty’s notebook, he had followed its diagrams as carefully as he could. It had been enough for neighborhood repairs and clever fixes in a fight. It was not enough to keep Zerdata’s old architecture reliable.

Raya found him late that evening, still staring at the actuator readings.

“You could ask Jax,” she said.

Kura looked up. “Master Jax?”

“You already know he restores older parts. He works with salvaged components every day. And I know he has offered training contracts to young mechanics before.”

“He doesn’t exactly seem like the kind of person who gives lessons for free.”

“Then take something useful in trade. We can manage the shop while you’re gone.”

Kura hesitated. The new repairs had kept the business steady, but leaving for days at a time felt different from taking a short trip. Raya read the worry on his face.

“We are paying the bills,” she said. “And the extra we’ve been setting aside is still there. You helped make that possible. Let us help you now.”

The next morning, Kura took Zerdata’s damaged actuator and the diagnostic tablet to District 7.

Master Jax’s scrapyard covered several long blocks, a layered landscape of sorted metal, dismantled chassis, stacked tires, and half-rebuilt machines. A crane creaked above the central yard. Sparks burst from a welding bay. The air smelled of hot steel and rain-soaked dust.

Jax stood under a corrugated awning, working on a thickly armored shoulder plate. His grey hair was tied back, and a pair of magnifying lenses sat pushed up on his forehead.

“You’re Ty Ato’s boy,” he said without looking around.

“Kura. I need help with an old AniBot chassis.”

Jax set down his tool and glanced at Zerdata. His expression changed by a fraction—not surprise, exactly, but recognition that he did not intend to explain.

“You want me to fix it?”

“I want to learn how to fix it.”

“Better answer.” Jax wiped his hands on a cloth. “What have you tried?”

Kura laid out the diagnostics and explained the actuator mismatch. Jax listened, then tapped the report with a calloused finger.

“You’re reading the failure correctly. You’re treating the parts like they’re separate problems. On an old frame, every replacement changes the load on the parts around it. You can’t learn that from a clean diagram.”

“So you’ll show me?”

“No.”

Kura blinked.

Jax pointed to a stack of unprocessed scrap. “I’ll teach you if you bring back the parts I need for my outstanding jobs. First contract is simple. A pair of intact actuator housings from the old rail yard. Don’t bring me crushed metal and call it close enough.”

“And the lesson?”

“You get it after you bring back something worth teaching with.”

Jax turned back to his shoulder plate. The conversation was over.

## 2. The First Runs

The nearest wasteland began beyond the city’s western salvage boundary, where old service roads gave way to rusted rail lines and windblown lots. It was not empty. The land was crowded with the leftovers of previous generations: collapsed freight frames, stripped power pylons, broken maintenance bots, and heaps of metal softened by years of heat and grit.

Kura and Zerdata went without the others on their first trip. Zerdata was not ready for a long fight, so Kura planned a short route through the nearest rail yard and packed a tool roll, water, a portable power cell, and a hand scanner. He had assumed that finding two intact actuator housings would be the hard part.

He found them before noon, buried under a tilted cargo rack.

Getting them out without damaging their alignment sleeves took the rest of the afternoon.

One housing slipped from the rack and struck the ground. Kura caught it before it rolled beneath a wrecked hauler, scraping his knuckles on the metal. Zerdata braced the rack while Kura worked the housing free a little at a time.

“You could have asked me to hold it,” Zerdata said.

“You are holding it.”

“I could have held it more efficiently.”

“I’ll put that in the report.”

Jax inspected the salvaged housings in silence when Kura returned. He ran a small gauge around each sleeve, checked the inner teeth, and set the better one aside.

“This one’s usable,” he said. “The other one has a hairline split.”

Kura frowned. “I didn’t see it.”

“That’s why you’re here.”

The first lesson was how to clean a salvaged component without stripping away the protective finish. Jax made Kura do it by hand, explaining what each solvent did and why the pitted surface could not be polished smooth without weakening its edge.

“Scrap keeps its history,” Jax said. “Your job is to find out which parts of that history matter.”

The second trip took two days. Kura searched an old freight depot for a pair of servo couplers. The depot’s upper platforms were unstable, and a heavy maintenance bot—still moving despite a missing sensor plate—rolled out of the dark when Kura disturbed its charging nest.

Its claws scraped sparks from the concrete. Zerdata stepped between it and Kura, but the worn shoulder actuator buckled under the first impact. Kura shouted for Zerdata to retreat, then used a length of loose cable to pull a hanging sheet of metal down between them. The bot turned toward the crash. Kura and Zerdata slipped around its flank and escaped through a side loading door.

They returned without the couplers. Jax listened to the story, then gave Kura a new coil of cable.

“You know what you did wrong?”

“I went too close before checking the area.”

“And?”

Kura thought it over. “I treated Zerdata’s damaged joint like it could take the same load as the other side.”

“Good. A machine can only compensate for the mechanic’s optimism for so long.”

Jax sent him back the next week. This time, Kura found the couplers in a toppled relay shed and returned without disturbing the old maintenance bot. He also brought home a worn gyroscope that was not on the contract list, thinking its housing might help stabilize Zerdata’s damaged hip.

Jax looked at it, then at Kura. “Now you’re starting to see the work.”

The weeks settled into a rhythm. Kura spent several days at the workshop, then one or two in the wasteland. Raya handled the daily jobs and kept him updated by message. Some evenings, Kura came home with a useful part. Others, he returned empty-handed, dusty, and carrying a new list of mistakes.

He learned to judge the memory of a metal’s bend, clean oxidized contacts without scouring their plating, and test actuator alignment with a weighted arm rather than trusting the visible markings. Jax was blunt about each failure, but he never made Kura repeat a mistake without explaining what he had missed.

When Kura applied the new methods to Zerdata, the improvement was small but real. A rebuilt actuator no longer fought the old control profile. Zerdata’s shoulder moved more smoothly; the power spikes softened. Kura fitted the gyroscope housing to the hip assembly and tuned it by hand until Zerdata could turn sharply without the frame rocking out of alignment.

“How does it feel?” Kura asked.

Zerdata stood from the bench and took a careful step, then another. It rotated its torso, shifted its weight, and raised both arms.

“More balanced,” it said. “Still not as fast as I would prefer.”

“I’ll tell the scrapyard you’re dissatisfied with their inventory.”

“Please do.”

## 3. Friends on the Trail

By the fourth week, Jax’s contracts had taken Kura farther from the city. One required a set of intact power regulators from a wind farm beyond the outer rail loop. Luk volunteered before Kura had finished describing the route.

“You need somebody who can carry the heavy stuff,” Luk said. “And keep you from trying to dismantle a tower while it’s still standing.”

“I wasn’t going to do that.”

“You were thinking about it.”

Luk brought Kabuto-Horn. The AniBot’s heavy frame made short work of shifting the fallen turbine panels, though it complained each time Luk asked it to crouch in the sand.

The regulators were inside a service shed with a damaged door. As Kura worked the lock, a pair of rogue maintenance units emerged from behind the turbine base. Their warning lights flashed as they charged, arms swinging to clear the area.

Kabuto-Horn met the first with a shoulder check that knocked it sideways into a mound of gravel. The second darted past and aimed for Kura. Zerdata pivoted to intercept, but the loose sand stole its footing.

“Left leg, now!” Luk called.

Kura saw the weight shift before the next strike. “Zerdata, drop!”

Zerdata sank low. The attacker’s arm swept over its head. Kabuto-Horn rushed in from the side, caught the maintenance unit around its waist, and flipped it onto its back. The first machine pushed itself up and lunged again. Luk drew Kabuto-Horn back; Zerdata kicked a loose turbine brace across its path. The unit stumbled over it, and Kabuto-Horn shoved it down beside its partner.

The scuffle lasted less than a minute. It left Kura’s hands shaking anyway.

“You called the drop before it swung,” Luk said as they secured the regulators. “That was good.”

“You set up the opening.”

“Yeah. We’re good at this.”

On the ride back, Luk talked about his dream of qualifying for the World Cup, then challenged Kura to promise they would meet in the finals. Kura said he would try. Luk groaned at the answer.

“You’re supposed to say yes.”

“Then yes.”

“That sounded like you’re agreeing to a repair estimate.”

The next expedition was supposed to be straightforward: scan a cluster of discarded chassis frames and recover the intact sensor arrays. Isabell came along with Swan-Grace, offering to map the area and watch the terrain while Kura worked.

She had wanted to see him since he returned from District 9. The feeling had only grown sharper in the weeks since the mountain trip. She kept remembering his hand held out to her in the dark, his voice telling her to stay behind him, and the way he had turned back to check that she was safe before running toward the station.

She did not know how to tell him that those moments had stayed with her. Every time she got close to saying something, she worried she would make things awkward between them—or that he would smile, misunderstand, and move on to talking about a machine.

So she asked to join the salvage trip instead.

“If you’re sure you don’t mind the dust,” Kura said.

“I don’t mind. Swan-Grace can scan the frames from above.”

She had prepared a route map and marked the more stable ground in advance. Kura thanked her, and Isabell tucked the words away as if they were more important than they were.

The sensor arrays were scattered across an old assembly yard. Swan-Grace skimmed between the frames, wings catching the wind, while its sensors picked up a faint signal beneath a collapsed roof.

“There’s something moving under there,” Isabell said. “Not a bot. It’s a repeating vibration.”

Kura and Luk cleared debris from the roof edge. The metal shifted suddenly, sending a cascade of panels down the slope. Isabell saw it before either boy did.

“Kura, move!”

Swan-Grace sprang forward, caught Kura around the torso, and pulled him clear as the panels crashed down. Luk ducked behind a concrete support. Dust rolled through the yard.

Kura coughed, then looked at Isabell. “You saved me.”

“Swan-Grace did.”

“You saw it coming.”

She tried to brush the dust from her sleeve. “I’m glad you’re okay.”

She wanted to say that she was glad because it was him. Instead, she checked the scanner again and told him the signal had stopped.

They found a damaged sensor unit trapped beneath the roof. It was not one of Jax’s requested parts, but its casing bore an old research stamp half-hidden under corrosion. Kura cleaned a corner with his thumb. The mark looked unfamiliar, yet it tugged at a memory of his father’s notebooks.

He bagged the unit carefully.

“You know what it is?” Isabell asked.

“Not yet.”

“Then we’ll figure it out.”

The ease with which she said *we* warmed him more than he expected. He looked at her a moment too long. Isabell noticed and turned quickly to help Luk pack the sensor arrays.

## 4. The Mark Beneath the Rust

By the start of the second month, Kura could rebuild a worn joint without Jax standing over his shoulder. He still made mistakes, but he understood why they happened. He learned to salvage what could be restored, reject what would fail under load, and adapt a modern part without forcing it to behave like something it was not.

The research-stamped sensor became the subject of his next lesson. Jax recognized the symbol as soon as Kura set it on the table.

He did not touch it.

“Where did you find this?”

“Assembly yard past the outer loop. It was under a collapsed roof.”

“You were lucky it wasn’t crushed.”

“What program is the mark from?”

Jax’s jaw tightened. “An old one.”

“You know more than that.”

“I know that stamp doesn’t belong on ordinary salvage.” He turned the unit with a gloved hand. “And I know you’ll get nothing useful out of it until you can restore it without breaking what’s left.”

Kura studied the corroded casing. “Can you tell me how?”

“No.” Jax pushed it back across the table. “But you’ve got the tools now. Try.”

It took Kura two days to open the unit without damaging its inner board. He cleaned each contact under magnification, replaced a cracked connector, and traced the surviving paths with a narrow probe. The old board resisted every modern adapter he tried. Zerdata listened as Kura muttered through the problem, occasionally pointing out a sound in the sensor’s faint test pulses that Kura had missed.

“There,” Zerdata said after the third attempt. “The return signal is uneven.”

Kura adjusted the probe. A small amber light flickered on the sensor board.

The tablet filled with fragments of old calibration data—most of it corrupted, some of it legible. Beneath the old research stamp, a second marking surfaced: a set of initials and a handwritten notation that matched the style of Ty Ato’s workshop records.

Kura stopped breathing for a moment.

Jax leaned over his shoulder. “You know those initials?”

“They’re my father’s.”

The room seemed to lose its background noise: crane, hammer, distant voices. Kura enlarged the fragment. It was not a message, only a partial equipment log and a location code he could not place.

“Was this his?” Isabell asked quietly. She had come to return the scanner she borrowed for the trip and had stayed when she saw Kura’s expression.

“I think he worked with this program. Maybe he was tracking these parts.”

Jax straightened. “Then keep the unit safe and don’t go chasing the location code alone. A scrap mark is a clue, not an invitation to get yourself buried under a bad decision.”

Kura nodded, though his thoughts were already moving ahead.

Isabell stayed beside him while he copied the surviving data. She did not ask him to explain his face or tell him how to feel. She just sat close enough that their shoulders nearly touched.

When Kura looked up, she gave him a small, steady smile.

“You don’t have to solve it tonight,” she said.

“I know.”

He wanted to tell her that he was glad she was there. Instead, he turned the tablet toward her and showed her the location code.

She leaned in to read it. “We can look into it when you’re ready.”

Again, that quiet *we*.

## 5. Jax’s Test

Jax’s final contract arrived near the end of the second month. He led Kura to a wrecked freight lifter at the edge of a dry ravine. Its main support actuator had been badly bent, the housing split, and its inner coil exposed to dust and rain. The job required a functioning actuator for one of Jax’s clients. It would be Kura’s responsibility to recover and restore it.

“No hints?” Kura asked.

“You’ve had two months of hints.”

“That’s not the same thing.”

“It is today.”

Jax waited in the shade of the transport while Kura and Zerdata assessed the wreck. The ravine floor was littered with scrap. The actuator had twisted under the lifter’s weight, and a section of its housing was pinned beneath the chassis.

Kura did not reach for the wrench right away. He inspected the load points, tested the frame’s movement, and checked the ground around the wreck for unstable pieces. Then he and Zerdata braced the chassis with two steel supports and used a hand winch to ease the housing free.

The actuator was worse than it had looked. The coil had been scraped, but its core was intact. One mounting tooth had sheared off; the inner sleeve had warped out of alignment.

“Could replace it,” Kura said, more to himself than to Jax. “But the client needs the original coupling. If I force a standard sleeve in, it’ll transfer the stress to the frame.”

He set about cleaning the coil, separating the salvageable segments from the damaged ones. He reshaped a replacement tooth from a matching piece of metal, then fitted it with a brace that would distribute the load along the housing. He checked the alignment with a weighted arm, made a small correction, then checked it again.

Jax watched without speaking.

The test came when Kura connected the actuator to a portable power cell. It turned once, stuttered, and stopped.

Kura frowned, but he did not rip it apart. He listened to the motor, checked the contact readings, and found the connector pin sitting a fraction too high. He adjusted it, secured the housing, and started the test again.

This time the actuator moved smoothly through its full range.

Kura looked at Jax.

“You did the work,” Jax said. “That’s the point.”

Back at the yard, Kura used the same method to rebuild Zerdata’s damaged hip and shoulder components. The new work did not make Zerdata a modern machine. Its frame remained old, its maximum output limited. But the joints moved cleanly, the power flow was stable, and Kura could recognize what needed attention before a fault became a failure.

Zerdata stood on the workshop floor and ran through a sequence of turns, steps, and short dodges. It finished with a quick pivot that would have thrown its balance off weeks earlier.

“How does it feel?” Kura asked.

“More like myself,” Zerdata said.

Kura let out a breath he had been holding. “That’s what I wanted.”

Raya had been watching from the office doorway. She smiled and set a cup of tea beside his tools. “You did good work.”

“Jax did a lot of the teaching.”

“He made you do the work,” she said. “There’s a difference.”

When Jax visited the workshop the following afternoon, he inspected the rebuilt joints and gave a single nod.

“You can maintain this chassis without me hovering over you now.”

“That was the deal.”

“The deal was training. What you do with it is your problem.” Jax glanced toward the salvaged sensor unit, still secured in a case on the shelf. “And keep your eyes open for more of those marks. If your father was involved, there’s a reason he left records.”

Kura nodded. The mystery had not become clearer, but it had become real enough to follow.

## 6. A Promise for the Road Ahead

The two months had changed more than Zerdata’s repair log. Kura had learned to work beyond the clean bench and the easy answers in a manual. He had learned to judge a machine by what remained usable, and to accept that a repaired part did not have to be new to be strong.

Luk began showing up at the workshop with maps of the World Cup qualification circuit and fresh challenges for Kura. He wanted to test Zerdata’s rebuilt movement as soon as Kura considered it safe.

They spread the circuit dates across the counter and chose practice sessions that would fit around customer work. Kura checked Zerdata’s repair log before agreeing to the first one. Jax had taught him how to keep the chassis working; now he needed to find out how well he could guide it through a match.

Isabell visited often, sometimes with a question about Swan-Grace’s sensors, sometimes with an excuse to bring food while Kura worked. She had grown more comfortable standing beside him, though she still felt a small flutter whenever he thanked her or leaned over to see what she was reading.

She never said what she wanted to say. Not yet.

At the end of a late shift, she found Kura closing the tool cabinet. “You’ll tell us when you start preparing for qualifiers, right?”

“Of course.”

“I want to come watch. And help, if you need me.”

“I’d like that.”

The simple answer made her smile all the way to the door.

Kura turned back to Zerdata, who was standing under the workshop lights with its restored joints moving quietly and evenly. On the shelf, the old sensor’s recovered initials waited beside Ty’s copied notes. The repair work was done, but the trail his father had left behind was only beginning to show itself.

Outside, the city’s evening traffic flowed past the shop. Kura shut off the bench lamp, already thinking about the next steps: World Cup qualifiers, the location code, and the questions his father had never been able to answer.

For the first time, he could maintain Zerdata’s chassis without waiting for someone else to show him what to do. He left Luk’s practice schedule beside the repair log, ready for the morning.
