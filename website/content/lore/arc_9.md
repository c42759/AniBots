## 1. The Champion Comes Home

Kura knew something was wrong when the transport stopped before it reached the workshop.

There were people outside the doors. Too many people, with too many cameras, and someone had parked a broadcast case in the space where the courier usually left damaged stabilizers.

Sammy stood on the front step with her arms folded.

“One question at a time,” she announced. “And nobody touches the shutter.”

“Are you a member of Kura Ato’s team?” a reporter asked.

“District Four,” Sammy replied, as if that explained the arrangement.

Spy stood beside her with his scanner tucked beneath his arm. “All interview requests go through me. Name, outlet, subject, and whether you brought refreshments.”

“He made that last part up,” Slow said.

“I’m establishing a professional standard.”

Slow held the workshop door open for a customer carrying an irrigation controller. Honey-Beam moved a little to let the man pass. Behind the counter, Raya was finishing a receipt with the determined calm of someone who had already explained six times that the workshop was still a business.

Luk leaned across Kura to look through the transport window.

“You’ve got staff.”

“I haven’t hired anyone.”

“That makes it even more impressive.”

Anda studied the crowd. “You should have entered through the service lane.”

“You could have said that before we stopped.”

“I wanted to see how long it would take you to notice.”

Zerdata shifted beside Kura. The temporary brace had been removed after the final engineering check at the Dome, but the frame still moved carefully. Its reserves had recovered enough for the journey home. The left hip and both shoulders would need a closer inspection.

“There are eleven active recording devices,” it said.

Kura stared at it. “Please stop counting.”

“Twelve.”

Sammy saw the transport door open.

“He’s here!” Spy shouted, abandoning his own interview system at once.

The cameras turned.

Kura had faced Soren with thousands of people watching. Somehow, crossing the short pavement to his own front door felt worse.

“Kura! How does it feel to bring the World Cup to District Four?”

“Good,” he said. “Very good.”

“Did you expect to win?”

“I hoped we could.”

“Was the final attack a secret technique developed by your father?”

His step slowed.

“We’re still reviewing what happened.”

Another microphone moved closer. “The footage shows you ordering Zerdata to stop. Did your AniBot ignore you?”

Zerdata’s ears lowered slightly.

Kura stopped walking.

“We lost communication during the match,” he said. “Zerdata is recovering. That comes first.”

For a moment, his voice was steady enough that the reporter lowered the microphone.

Then someone behind her asked, “Will the championship prize go toward a new workshop? Or are you considering a corporate team?”

“I haven’t—”

“And who was the girl beside you after the final?”

Kura blinked.

Isabell, who had been approaching with Swan-Grace, stopped on the pavement.

“That’s Isa,” Kura said.

“Someone special?”

Luk made a sound suspiciously like a cough.

“She helped us,” Kura answered. “With the sensors. And the chassis. And during the tournament.”

Anda turned his face away, unsuccessfully hiding a smile.

“I see,” the reporter said. “So you two are—”

“Coming inside,” Raya said from the doorway. “My son has just come home.”

She handed Kura the irrigation controller.

He took it automatically.

“This is due tomorrow,” she added.

The cameras caught the World Champion looking down at a broken household machine with obvious relief.

“I can do tomorrow.”

“Wonderful. Five more minutes of questions, then he’s off duty.”

Sammy immediately raised one hand. “You heard the manager.”

By the time Kura reached the counter, Luk was grinning so broadly that Kura wanted to shut the storeroom door on him.

“With the sensors,” Luk repeated.

“She did help with the sensors.”

“A precise answer,” Anda said. “You handled that beautifully.”

Outside, Isabell stared through the doorway, her eyes wide and her cheeks burning.

Luk leaned toward her as she passed.

“You know you need to tell him. He doesn’t understand what’s between the lines.”

“You could stop making it worse.”

“I could.”

She elbowed him gently and went inside.

The reporters used every one of Raya’s five minutes. Kura thanked his friends, thanked District 4, and declined to demonstrate anything. Zerdata answered a question about the V2 chassis by listing three joints that required maintenance. The reporter did not ask a follow-up.

At last, the broadcast cases were packed away. The last camera turned toward the canal road.

Raya closed the door.

For the first time since the final, Kura heard the workshop without a crowd behind it: the ceiling fan, the cooling rack, a loose panel tapping softly beside the window.

He set the controller on the bench.

Raya looked at him, then at Zerdata.

“Welcome home.”

Kura stepped into her arms before she could say anything else.

## 2. Enough Chairs

Raya decided they were celebrating.

“We’ve spent months worrying about the next match,” she said. “Tonight, we can have dinner.”

Sammy nodded. “Good idea. Slow, bring the extra chairs.”

Raya looked at her.

Sammy looked back with complete confidence.

“We’ll help,” Slow offered.

“We already are helping,” Spy said, picking up a stack of plates.

Raya watched him take twice as many as he could safely carry, then relieved him of half.

“Wash your hands first. All three of you.”

Within an hour, the central worktable had become a dinner table. Clean cloth covered the scratched surface. Customer parts had been moved to the side benches, and Kura’s trophy stood between a bowl of rice and the old lamp with the repaired switch.

It looked slightly too large for the room.

Kura liked it better there than on the platform.

Dr. Thorne arrived with Holy-Vanguard shortly before the food was ready. Jax followed, carrying a small wrapped package that he set beside Zerdata’s elbow.

“Replacement shoulder bushings,” he said. “You’ve probably flattened the ones you fitted.”

“You watched?” Kura asked.

“I saw the joints take a load they weren’t designed for.”

Raya smiled. “He watched every match.”

Jax took a chair. “Food getting cold?”

At the other end of the table, Sammy had cornered Anda.

“I always knew he was a World Champion,” she said. “Since the first day I saw him.”

Anda set down his cup. “Was that before or after you challenged him three against one?”

“I was testing him.”

“We’ve been best friends since we were kids,” Spy added.

“You are still kids,” Anda said. “And you tried to shoot at him.”

“Cyan was doing the shooting.”

Slow considered this. “Kura helped fix Honey-Beam after we lost.”

Sammy’s expression shifted. She picked at the edge of her napkin.

“Yes,” she said. “He did.”

For once, Spy had nothing to add.

Across the room, Kabuto-Horn was showing Holy-Vanguard its replacement guard hinge. Swan-Grace stood beside Zerdata near the open workshop doors, where the evening air helped cool the frame.

“Your left shoulder is settling unevenly,” Swan-Grace observed.

“I am aware.”

“You keep raising it whenever someone looks at you.”

Zerdata lowered the shoulder.

“That is better,” Swan-Grace said.

Kura heard the exchange and set his plate aside.

“Does it hurt?”

“Only when I move it too far.”

“Then stop demonstrating that you’re fine.”

Zerdata’s ears turned toward him. “You have been doing that all evening.”

Kura glanced at the table, where Luk was recounting the opening round with enough additional drama to make it sound like a siege.

“I’m happy,” Kura said.

“I know.”

“I’m also thinking about the final.”

“So am I.”

For a moment, they listened to the room together.

“We can think about it tomorrow,” Kura said.

“Dr. Thorne has already opened the telemetry.”

At the table, the doctor looked up from his slate. Raya held out her hand.

Thorne surrendered it.

“Tomorrow,” she said.

Luk raised his cup. “To Kura and Zerdata.”

Everyone joined him. Spy knocked his cup against Sammy’s too hard and spilled a little water. Slow caught the bowl beside it before it tipped. Anda moved the trophy farther from both of them.

Kura looked around the table. These were the people who had carried broken parts home with him, argued over diagrams, waited outside arenas, and made sure he ate when he forgot.

“Thank you,” he said.

Luk waved that away. “You’re still losing the rematch.”

Kura smiled. “You’ll have to get to the final next time.”

The room erupted.

For a little while, the trophy was the only thing from the Grand Dome that mattered.

## 3. What the Instruments Recorded

Three days later, the worktable was covered in diagnostics again.

Kura had replaced Zerdata’s shoulder bushings and inspected the hip assembly with Jax. The damage was repairable: compressed mounts, stressed insulation, and fine scoring where the harmonic supports had shifted. Nothing suggested that the chassis had been built to carry the final attack comfortably.

That made the recording harder to understand.

Thorne laid three traces beside one another: Zerdata’s maintenance log, the Handler link recording, and the arena telemetry released with the final match report.

Luk leaned over the table. Anda sat opposite him. Isabell had arranged Swan-Grace’s scanner readings beside Kura’s notes.

“The highest figures are unreliable,” Thorne said.

Kura frowned. “You think the sensors were wrong?”

“I think they reached their limits. Once a sensor saturates, the number on the screen cannot tell us how far beyond that limit the event went.”

He enlarged the final usable section before the traces flattened.

“These measurements are more useful. Several independent systems agree here. Even the lowest consistent estimate exceeds the predictions in my family’s surviving models.”

“By how much?” Anda asked.

Thorne took off his spectacles.

“Enough that I would have rejected the calculation before seeing the match.”

Nobody spoke for a moment.

Kura looked at the recording of his own hand trying to lower Zerdata’s output. Each rejected command had a timestamp. There were more than he remembered.

“The link stayed connected,” Isabell said quietly. “It was transmitting. Zerdata just wasn’t answering it.”

“I could not hear him,” Zerdata said.

It stood beside the table, fully repaired but unusually still.

“What could you hear?” Thorne asked.

“At first, Tigris. Then the tone.”

“The one in the recovered fragments?”

“Yes. After that, the testing systems. Other cores answering.”

Thorne brought up one of the broken images from Kura’s display. White light, a cradle, the edge of a test console.

“Is this what you remember?”

“Some of it. The recording makes it look as if I was looking across a room. I wasn’t. I had no eyes.”

Kura put down his stylus.

“How did you know the room was there?”

“The equipment told me. Temperature. Distance. Changes in sound. Different systems gave me different pieces.” Zerdata paused. “Sometimes the researchers changed the input and waited to see whether I could still recognize it.”

“And the other cores?” Luk asked.

“They answered differently. One signal was very direct. One was steady. Another changed the pattern before sending it back.”

“Do you know who they were?”

“I knew then.”

The answer was quieter than Kura expected.

Thorne let the image fade.

“A recovered memory can be incomplete without being false,” he said. “We should record what you remember without making you fill the gaps.”

Zerdata’s fingers opened and closed once.

“I don’t know why I said the attack’s name.”

Kura looked up.

“Anima Limit Breaker,” Zerdata continued. “I remember saying it. I cannot find a command that explains it. I cannot tell you how to do it again.”

“Did you choose to fire?”

“I remember Tigris coming toward me. Then you were beside me.”

Kura looked down at the table. There had been only seconds between those moments, but a great deal had happened inside them.

“Do you remember being angry?”

“Yes.” Zerdata’s ears lowered. “I wanted Tigris to stop speaking. Then I wanted it to stop getting up.”

Luk’s expression lost its usual humor.

“I did not want to stop hearing you,” Zerdata told Kura.

“I know.”

“You kept calling.”

“I’ll keep calling.”

Thorne waited until Zerdata’s posture eased before turning back to the traces.

“The memory sectors opened during the same interval as the output increase. That gives us a sequence. It does not yet tell us whether one caused the other.”

Anda nodded. “Or whether both came from something else.”

“Exactly.”

Kura saved the three traces together. He had wanted the logs to turn the final into something he could inspect, understand, and repair.

So far, they had mostly shown him how much he had missed.

## 4. Nothing Happened

They booked an enclosed test bay in District 5.

Thorne insisted on instruments that could record independently of Zerdata’s own interface. Kura brought the maintenance kit, checked the emergency isolation system, and set the ordinary output limits below tournament levels. They were testing whether any part of the unfamiliar response could be observed safely, beginning with the smallest possible attempt.

There was no opponent on the floor.

Zerdata faced an empty target enclosure. Kura stood where it could see him.

“Ready?”

“Ready.”

Zerdata raised its hands in the position recorded before the beam.

The overhead sensors tracked the movement. A fan started somewhere behind the wall.

Nothing happened.

Luk glanced at the monitor, then back at Zerdata.

“Maybe the name?”

Zerdata held the pose for another second.

“Anima Limit Breaker.”

The fan continued running.

“I thought it was worth asking,” Luk said.

For the next hour, they tested the movements separately. Arm angle. Foot position. The ordinary harmonic emitter at low power, then at the upper end of its usual range. Every pulse behaved like a normal Sonic Pulse Cannon discharge. The right claw produced its familiar high-frequency vibration. No green-yellow light appeared.

Thorne checked the recorders after every attempt.

Zerdata began to watch him do it.

“I can try again.”

“You have already repeated that sequence,” Thorne said.

“The result may change.”

Kura lowered his slate. “How does your shoulder feel?”

“Functional.”

“That wasn’t the question.”

Zerdata lowered its arms.

“Warm.”

Kura ended the cycle.

During the break, Isabell played the recovered communication tone at a soft level, with Zerdata’s agreement. Its ears turned at once.

Kura watched the readings.

“Anything?”

“Familiar,” Zerdata said. “But I remember the recording now, too. I cannot tell which part I am recognizing.”

Isabell stopped playback.

They tried one final low-power sequence after the frame cooled. It produced a clean, ordinary pulse and nothing more.

The equipment shut down around them.

“The final was different,” Luk said. “The fight, the pressure, what Tigris was saying—”

He stopped when he saw Zerdata turn toward the empty floor.

“I know,” Kura said.

There was a pause.

“We’re not going to make you angry to see what happens.”

Zerdata looked back at him.

“It could be relevant.”

“It could. You also stopped hearing me, and the arena’s emergency halt failed. We don’t understand enough to put you through that again.”

Thorne closed the recorder case. “We can investigate a condition without recreating the whole event.”

Anda checked the last trace. “Today’s result is still useful. The stance and ordinary emitter sequence do not reproduce it.”

“That sounds better than ‘nothing happened,’” Luk said.

“It is more precise.”

On the way out, Zerdata walked beside Kura rather than ahead of him.

“Do you think I can still fight?”

Kura stopped by the bay doors.

“I think we need to understand what happened before entering another tournament.”

“That was not what I meant.”

Kura looked at its face.

“You’re still my partner.”

The ears lifted a little.

“Even when the test fails?”

“You watched me build two chassis designs that didn’t work.”

“The first one was very heavy.”

“Thank you.”

“The second one overheated.”

“I remember.”

Zerdata’s steps grew less careful as they went down the corridor together.

## 5. A Name in the Margin

That evening, Kura returned to Ty’s notebook.

He had used its diagrams to restore the first frame, then to build the V2. He knew where the acoustic curves were, which pages held the older coupling measurements, and where his father’s handwriting became cramped around a calculation he had changed three times.

Now he read the references beneath the diagrams.

Most pointed to other pages. A few ended in short codes that he had treated as labels for Ty’s experiments.

One appeared beneath several synchronization drawings.

**RCA — HORIZON / INSTRUMENTATION**

Below it, in smaller letters:

*Reference rig. Full measurements retained on site.*

Kura placed the notebook beside the terminal and searched the facility names in the records from Zero.

Research Center Ato appeared in a later research directory.

Old New Zealand.

He stared at the map for a long moment before calling Thorne.

The doctor came the next morning. Raya sat with them while Kura laid the repeated references across the table.

“I knew Ty worked there,” Thorne said. “He sent me selected calculations from the center. I never saw his complete engineering record.”

“Why didn’t we look there before?”

“Because the material he shared with me concerned compatible chassis design. Until the final, I did not know there was an energy event we needed to compare against his original measurements.”

Thorne touched the reference beneath the curve.

“This is more specific than anything I have. An instrument rig, a receiving station, and a record number.”

Raya leaned toward the notebook.

“Horizon Port,” she said. “That name was on some of his travel receipts.”

“You remember it?”

“I remember asking why research instruments needed so many freight forms.” Her smile faded. “He said the delicate parts took more paperwork than the heavy ones.”

Kura turned another page. A second reference matched the same station and a different test date.

He had looked at those marks dozens of times. He had needed to know how the parts fit together, so he had read everything around them as instructions for a build. Now he could see his father referring to work done elsewhere.

They spent the next two days checking what could be reached before arranging a visit.

Anda found the current facility registration. Research Center Ato remained officially active, with access restrictions still in place. Its public directory offered old division names and unattended contact addresses. It provided almost nothing about ongoing work.

“Active on a register,” Anda said. “That tells us the site was never formally closed. It does not tell us who is there.”

Thorne submitted a request for the instrumentation records using his research credentials and the test numbers from Ty’s notebook. An automated system acknowledged his application. After a long delay, it authorized a limited inspection of the Horizon receiving and calibration annex.

The core-research buildings required a separate clearance. No staff member answered their inquiries.

“We start with the annex,” Thorne said. “If the original records are there, we can compare them properly before deciding where to search next.”

Luk arrived with a travel bag before Kura asked him.

“You’re coming?”

“You carried an entire World Cup home. I’m carrying a bag.”

Anda would remain in Neo Metropolis to examine the records as they were recovered. Isabell offered to compare the new measurements with the final’s recordings and help Raya keep the workshop’s incoming messages under control.

There were still far too many of those.

Before Kura left, she brought him a compact case of cleaned sensor leads.

“You forgot these when you packed for Zero.”

“I remembered them eventually.”

“When you needed them.”

He took the case. “Thank you.”

Isabell glanced toward the stairs, where Luk was loudly asking Raya whether a second food bag counted as essential equipment.

“When you come back,” she said, “could we go somewhere?”

Kura looked up.

“The canal market, maybe. Just for an afternoon.”

“Do you need parts?”

She closed her eyes briefly.

“No, Kura. I’d just like to go with you.”

He was quiet for a moment. Then his expression softened.

“I’d like that.”

“All right.”

She smiled and let go of the sensor case.

From the stairs, Luk caught her eye. She pointed at him before he could speak.

He raised both hands and returned to negotiating over the food.

## 6. Horizon Port

The journey to Old New Zealand carried them far beyond the routes Kura knew.

By the final coastal connection, the city’s layered towers had given way to dark bays and ridges streaked with snow. Research Center Ato occupied a stretch of coastline below the mountains, its buildings separated by water, slopes, and broad areas of recovering forest.

Horizon Port stood at the water’s edge. Loading cranes rose above empty berths. Farther inland, glass-fronted research blocks followed a road toward the mountains. A dish antenna turned slowly on a distant cliff.

The port lights were on.

There was nobody waiting beneath them.

“For an active research center,” Luk said, “it’s very quiet.”

Zerdata’s ears swept toward the loading sheds.

“Pumps. Ventilation. A maintenance cart in the eastern building.”

“People?”

“None that I can hear.”

Thorne checked the entry terminal. His authorization opened the pedestrian gate and lit a path toward the instrumentation annex. The display welcomed them by name, then advised them to contact the duty office if assistance was required.

The duty office did not answer.

They recorded their arrival and followed the lit path. Holy-Vanguard walked beside Thorne, its pale shield arm angled away from the narrow railing. Kabuto-Horn carried the equipment case. Zerdata stayed near Kura.

The annex smelled faintly of salt and filtered air. Dust lay along the window edges. The work surfaces were clear, and protective covers remained over several calibration machines.

Kura checked the room before connecting anything. The external power sockets tested clean. A cabinet beside the main rig held maintenance records and printed equipment certificates.

One certificate bore Ty’s signature.

Kura held it by the edges.

It was only an inspection form. A date, a list of measured tolerances, a handwritten correction where somebody had entered the wrong connector rating.

He could picture his father finding that mistake.

“Kura?” Luk said.

“He worked right here.”

Luk came to stand beside him.

Through the window, a crane shifted slightly in the wind. Somewhere below the floor, a pump completed its cycle.

Kura put the certificate down carefully and opened the next drawer.

The rig’s local archive contained the record numbers from the notebook. Thorne’s clearance let them retrieve those files, along with their calibration history and attached engineering drawings.

At first, the diagrams looked almost disappointingly familiar.

“The torso supports,” Kura said. “The original ones.”

“Before he altered the heat channels,” Thorne replied.

They placed the drawing beside Kura’s copy of the home notebook. Here was the same design earlier in its development: a heavier support brace, a differently placed damper, an emitter connection Ty had crossed out in the later version.

The records showed repeated tests of how a chassis carried changing harmonic input. A conventional controller tried to smooth irregular signals into a fixed pattern. Ty’s experiments allowed the timing to vary and measured whether the structure could remain stable around it.

One test series failed almost at once. Another held until the input changed direction. A third stayed stable through several variations before a mounting bracket cracked.

“He broke a lot of brackets,” Luk said.

Kura found a note beside the failed result.

*Input remained coherent. Mounting geometry unsuitable. Rebuild support; retain signal behavior.*

He read it twice.

“He didn’t assume the signal was wrong because the part failed.”

Thorne nodded. “He was trying to find out what it required.”

The prototype folder extended beyond the fox-shaped chassis. There were unfinished interfaces, load-bearing structures, and several unlabeled core housings. Some were little more than dimensions and questions. None identified a current owner or a living Ancient chip.

Zerdata studied a page of response curves.

“Does this explain the attack?”

“Not yet,” Kura said.

They compared Ty’s measurements with the final’s usable telemetry. Certain timing changes resembled the behavior Ty had been testing, but the scale was entirely different. His records described a frame adapting to variable input. They did not describe a beam that shook the Grand Dome.

Thorne checked the accompanying research note.

“He was asking whether Ancient architecture could continue changing its response without losing coherence,” he said. “Resonance, adaptation, and the machinery built around them. These were connected problems to him.”

“Did he solve them?”

“He solved enough to build your first body,” Thorne told Zerdata. “These files do not tell us how much further he got.”

Several references pointed inland toward Ato Labs, the core-research division. Those files were outside their present clearance. The annex held transferred test results and equipment documentation; it was not Ty’s complete research archive.

They spent the rest of the day collecting what they could access and checking every result against its source. By evening, Kura’s eyes ached.

The last folder contained comparison material attached to an experimental synchronization series.

Its dates were much older than the tests around it.

“Luk,” Kura said. “Come look at this.”

## 7. The Ones Who Answered

There were four traces on the screen.

Each began with the same repeated test tone. After it, the responses separated into distinct patterns: brief and direct, low and steady, intricate with variations, then a quick sequence that appeared to listen before returning a pulse.

Zerdata stepped closer.

“That one.”

Kura followed its hand to the fourth trace.

“Yours?”

“Yes.”

Thorne opened the source information. The attachment was a historical communication study from Research Center Zero. Ty had imported it as reference material for his synchronization work.

The fourth response was labeled **G0-004 — VULPES ZERDA**.

Kura looked at the other labels.

**G0-001. G0-002. G0-003.**

“Agon,” Luk said. “Biggon. Cygon.”

Zerdata’s ears lifted toward the speaker, though the file was still silent.

“May we play it?” Thorne asked.

Zerdata nodded.

They used the soft playback level from the test bay. The initiating tone sounded first. The responses followed, translated into audible pulses by the old equipment.

Kura watched Zerdata’s face rather than the readings.

There was no flare of green or yellow. No sudden rise in motor pitch.

Only a small turn of the head when the third pattern changed.

“It did that before,” Zerdata said.

“Cygon?”

“It would change something and wait for me to notice.”

Luk leaned closer to the screen. “That sounds annoying.”

“I think I liked it.”

Kura felt the words settle more heavily than any of the measurements they had recovered.

Thorne stopped the playback after the fourth response.

“These may be some of the signals you remembered in the final.”

“Some,” Zerdata agreed. “There were other tests. Other responses. I cannot put them all in order.”

“You don’t have to.”

Beside the imported study was a short assessment in Ty’s research file. It did not describe a meeting. It did not say where any of the three Primordials could be found. It compared the surviving written reports with the original response traces and ended with a recommendation.

> G0-001, G0-002 and G0-003 developed through direct interaction with the original experimental systems. Their retained experience may resolve questions the archived models cannot.
>
> If the three remain operational, prioritize direct testimony over reconstructed assumptions.

Thorne sat down slowly.

Kura looked at him. “You’ve spent your life studying those models.”

“Yes.”

“And Dad thought they were missing something.”

“So do I.” Thorne looked at Zerdata. “Especially now.”

They stayed until the annex lights shifted to their night setting. Beyond the glass, the inland road had become a row of distant white lamps leading toward the dark research buildings.

“Do we go there tomorrow?” Luk asked.

Thorne checked the access map. “I have requested access to Ato Labs. While we wait for a response, we can compare what we found here and check the route in daylight.”

Kura saved the final attachment and checked that the copy had completed. They would need the workshop’s championship logs beside these records, along with the comparison work Isabell and Anda had already begun.

He checked the annex terminal’s network connection.

The Internet link was still active.

“Let’s call home.”

Before he opened the call, Zerdata asked to hear the third response once more.

Kura played it.

The pattern changed near the end. Zerdata answered with a quiet pulse of its own.

Nothing answered from the speaker.

“Was that right?” Kura asked.

“I don’t know.”

He saved that response too, with Zerdata’s permission.

## 8. A Light in the Office

Raya answered the video call from behind the workshop counter.

For a moment, the camera showed only the ceiling and the edge of the parts ledger. Then she propped the device against a tool case, and the familiar room settled into view.

“Kura? Can you hear me?”

“Clearly.”

“Good. Isabell, they’re calling.”

Isabell hurried into view with a tablet beneath one arm. Behind her, Swan-Grace carried a shallow tray of labeled components toward the storage shelves.

“You found something?” she asked.

“Several things,” Kura said. “We’re sending the files now.”

Luk pulled a second chair toward the annex terminal. Thorne sat beside him, and Zerdata moved into the camera’s view at Kura’s shoulder.

Raya’s expression softened when she saw them together.

“You all look tired.”

“We’ve been reading Dad’s test records,” Kura said.

He shared the engineering drawings first, showing her the original torso supports and the calibration certificate with Ty’s signature. Raya leaned closer to the screen.

“He always crossed his sevens like that,” she said.

Kura looked at the handwritten correction again.

“I’d forgotten.”

She was quiet for a moment, then asked him to show her the next page.

They explained the synchronization experiments, the failed mounting brackets, and the differences between the measurements Ty had recorded and the output seen in the World Cup final.

“So these records still don’t explain the beam?” Isabell asked.

“No,” Thorne replied. “They show that Ty was studying the relationship between the core’s changing responses and the frame carrying them. We have more of his engineering work now, but nothing here describes Anima Limit Breaker.”

“The original measurements should help us check the comparison,” she said. “Anda has already started an index. I’ll send these to him as well.”

Kura brought up the historical communication study.

“We knew the Primordials saved the seven cores,” he said. “Now we know they were part of the tests Zerdata remembers. Dad thought their experience could explain things the reports couldn’t.”

The three designations appeared beside the traces.

Agon. Biggon. Cygon.

“Do you remember them now?” Raya asked Zerdata.

“Pieces,” it replied. “Cygon changed a signal. I remember trying to answer it. I do not remember everything that came before or after.”

“But you recognized it.”

“Yes.”

Raya smiled gently. “Then that’s something you didn’t have yesterday.”

Zerdata looked at the traces again.

“I would like to hear them answer.”

Kura rested a hand against its forearm. “We’ll keep looking.”

As the files finished transferring, something moved behind Isabell. A delivery unit stood beside the workbench, its open casing surrounded by neatly arranged trays. Beyond it, two more customer machines waited near the storage door.

Kura leaned toward the camera.

“Is that the courier from the canal market?”

Raya followed his gaze. “Its stabilizer started slipping again.”

“I thought the replacement would last longer.”

“It has been running extra routes.”

“And the loader behind it?”

“Came in this morning.”

Kura looked at the stack of repair cases beside the counter.

“We’ve already been away for days. If we stay here longer, all that work is going to pile up.”

“Kura,” Isabell said, “don’t worry about the workshop. I’m helping your mother organize everything while you’re away.”

She lifted her tablet and turned it toward the camera. The repair list had been sorted by delivery date, with separate notes for parts already available and jobs waiting on an order.

“I’ve labeled the incoming machines, checked which components we have, and moved the urgent requests to the top. Swan-Grace is helping with the smaller deliveries. Anything that needs you will be ready for you when you come back.”

“You’ve done all that?”

“Your mother showed me the system.”

“Isabell made it easier to use,” Raya said. “And I’m giving customers honest collection dates. Nobody is being promised a repair you haven’t had time to do.”

Kura studied the list. The work was still there, but it had become something he could picture returning to, rather than a growing heap beyond his reach.

“Thank you, Isa.”

She smiled. “Find what you went there for. We’re managing here.”

Luk leaned into the camera. “If you organize his tools too, he’ll never find anything again.”

“I know where his tools go.”

“That’s what makes it dangerous.”

Kura laughed, then glanced around the office. The sound faded quickly into the empty corridor beyond the door.

Raya noticed.

“Have you spoken to anyone at the center?”

“No,” Kura said.

“No one at all?”

“Not since we arrived,” Luk replied. “The gate let us in. The terminal knew about the inspection. But the duty office never answered.”

Kura turned the camera toward the room. A few computers glowed beneath the dimmed ceiling lights. Through the inner window, status lamps blinked on a server cabinet in the adjoining equipment room.

“Some computers are still working,” he said. “The local archive is available, and a few servers are running. There’s power, ventilation, even an Internet connection.”

He turned the camera back.

“But there are no signs of anyone using the place. No staff. No recent work left on the desks. We haven’t heard or seen another human.”

“The buildings we passed look abandoned,” Thorne said. “Yet the center’s registration remains active, and its automated systems continue to operate.”

Isabell frowned. “If everyone left, why wasn’t it deactivated?”

“That’s what I’d like to know,” Kura said.

“Could the machines just have kept running?” Raya asked.

“Some of them could,” Thorne replied. “That would explain the equipment. It would not explain why the facility was never formally closed.”

“Or why nobody answers for it,” Luk added.

Thorne looked toward the server cabinet. “We should check the administrative records when we can. At present, we don’t know whether anyone is still responsible for the site.”

For a moment, everyone listened to the ventilation through the call.

“Where will you sleep?” Raya asked.

“There’s an office beside the annex entrance,” Kura said. “Dry floor, a door we can secure, and enough space for the sleeping rolls. We’ll stay together.”

“And take turns keeping watch,” Luk said. “We’re not all going to sleep at once in a place we haven’t checked.”

Raya studied them, then nodded.

“Send me the building and room number before you settle in. Call again in the morning.”

“We will.”

Isabell looked at Kura. “Try to actually get some sleep.”

“I’ll try.”

“That means closing the notebook.”

Luk pointed at the screen. “Thank you. Someone had to say it.”

They ended the call after agreeing on the next check-in. For a few seconds, the dark screen reflected the four figures gathered around it.

Then Kura stood and began packing the equipment.

The office was small, with two desks, a filing cabinet, and a window overlooking the covered path to the annex. They checked the adjoining rooms and the nearest exit before carrying their bags inside. Luk tested the door catch. Holy-Vanguard inspected the corridor with Thorne while Kabuto-Horn helped shift a desk far enough to make room for the sleeping rolls.

Kura sent Raya their location and set his device to sound if she called.

They divided the night into watches. Luk and Kabuto-Horn would take the first, Thorne and Holy-Vanguard the middle, and Kura and Zerdata the last. Whoever rested would keep their equipment close enough to reach without crossing the room.

Luk sat beside the door with his back against the wall.

“Go on. Before I change my mind and give you the whole night.”

Kura sat on his sleeping roll. Through the window, he could see the lamps along the inland road. Somewhere beyond them stood Ato Labs.

He opened Ty’s notebook to mark the references they needed in the morning.

Luk cleared his throat.

Kura looked up.

“Isa said close it.”

“I’m only marking a page.”

“You’ve been marking pages for twenty minutes.”

Thorne had already removed his spectacles. “The records will still be there after you sleep.”

Kura slipped a paper marker between the pages and shut the cover.

Zerdata settled beside him, its ears angled toward the corridor.

“Wake me for our turn,” Kura said.

“I will.”

The office light clicked off. A thin strip of illumination remained beneath the door, broken now and then by Kabuto-Horn shifting beside Luk.

Farther inside the annex, the servers continued their steady hum.

Kura listened until he could distinguish it from the wind outside. There were no footsteps beyond their room, no voices, no sound of a researcher working late.

He pulled his jacket over his shoulders and lay down.

For now, Luk was watching the door. In a few hours, it would be his turn.
